Motion Equations for Constant Acceleration in One Dimension

When the acceleration does not change, four equations tie time, position, velocity and acceleration together. Each problem is a matter of knowing three of the quantities and picking the equation that gives the fourth.

We might know that the greater the acceleration of, say, a car moving away from a stop sign, the greater the displacement in a given time. But we have not developed a specific equation that relates acceleration and displacement. In this section, we develop some convenient equations for kinematic relationships, starting from the definitions of displacement, velocity, and acceleration already covered.

Notation: t\ktt, x\kxx, v\kvv, a\kaa

First, let us make some simplifications in notation. Taking the initial time to be zero, as if time is measured with a stopwatch, is a great simplification. Since elapsed time is Δt=tf−t0\kdt = t_f - t_0Δt=tf​−t0​, taking t0=0t_0 = 0t0​=0 means that Δt=tf\kdt = t_fΔt=tf​, the final time on the stopwatch. When initial time is taken to be zero, we use the subscript 0 to denote initial values of position and velocity. That is, x0\kxox0​ is the initial position and v0\kvov0​ is the initial velocity. We put no subscripts on the final values. That is, t\ktt is the final time, x\kxx is the final position, and v\kvv is the final velocity. This gives a simpler expression for elapsed time—now, Δt=t\kdt = \ktΔt=t. It also simplifies the expression for displacement, which is now Δx=x−x0\kdx = \kx - \kxoΔx=x−x0​. Also, it simplifies the expression for change in velocity, which is now Δv=v−v0\kdv = \kv - \kvoΔv=v−v0​. To summarize, using the simplified notation, with the initial time taken to be zero,

Δt=tΔx=x−x0Δv=v−v0\begin{aligned}\kdt &= \kt\\ \kdx &= \kx - \kxo\\ \kdv &= \kv - \kvo\end{aligned}ΔtΔxΔv​=t=x−x0​=v−v0​​

where the subscript 0 denotes an initial value and the absence of a subscript denotes a final value in whatever motion is under consideration.

Constant acceleration

We now make the important assumption that acceleration is constant. This assumption allows us to avoid using calculus to find instantaneous acceleration. Since acceleration is constant, the average and instantaneous accelerations are equal. That is,

aˉ=a=constant,\kab = \ka = \text{constant},aˉ=a=constant,

so we use the symbol a\kaa for acceleration at all times. Assuming acceleration to be constant does not seriously limit the situations we can study nor degrade the accuracy of our treatment. For one thing, acceleration is constant in a great number of situations. Furthermore, in many other situations we can accurately describe motion by assuming a constant acceleration equal to the average acceleration for that motion. Finally, in motions where acceleration changes drastically, such as a car accelerating to top speed and then braking to a stop, the motion can be considered in separate parts, each of which has its own constant acceleration.

Displacement from average velocity

Solving for Displacement (Δx\kdxΔx) and Final Position (x\kxx) from Average Velocity when Acceleration (a\kaa) is Constant

To get our first two new equations, we start with the definition of average velocity:

vˉ=ΔxΔt.\kvb = \frac{\kdx}{\kdt}.vˉ=ΔtΔx​.

Substituting the simplified notation for Δx\kdxΔx and Δt\kdtΔt yields

vˉ=x−x0t.\kvb = \frac{\kx - \kxo}{\kt}.vˉ=tx−x0​​.

Solving for x\kxx yields

x=x0+vˉt,\kx = \kxo + \kvb\kt,x=x0​+vˉt,

where the average velocity is

vˉ=v0+v2  (constant a).\kvb = \frac{\kvo + \kv}{2}\;(\text{constant }\ka).vˉ=2v0​+v​(constant a).

The equation vˉ=v0+v2\kvb = \frac{\kvo + \kv}{2}vˉ=2v0​+v​ reflects the fact that, when acceleration is constant, v\kvv is just the simple average of the initial and final velocities. For example, if you steadily increase your velocity (that is, with constant acceleration) from 30 to 60 km/h, then your average velocity during this steady increase is 45 km/h. Using the equation vˉ=v0+v2\kvb = \frac{\kvo + \kv}{2}vˉ=2v0​+v​ to check this, we see that

vˉ=v0+v2=30 km/h+60 km/h2=45 km/h,\kvb = \frac{\kvo + \kv}{2} = \frac{30\ \text{km/h} + 60\ \text{km/h}}{2} = 45\ \text{km/h},vˉ=2v0​+v​=230 km/h+60 km/h​=45 km/h,

which seems logical.

Example 2.8 · Calculating Displacement: How Far does the Jogger Run?

A jogger runs down a straight stretch of road with an average velocity of 4.00 m/s for 2.00 min. What is his final position, taking his initial position to be zero?

Strategy

Draw a sketch. The final position x\kxx is given by the equation

x=x0+vˉt.\kx = \kxo + \kvb\kt.x=x0​+vˉt.

To find x\kxx, we identify the values of x0\kxox0​, vˉ\kvbvˉ, and t\ktt from the statement of the problem and substitute them into the equation.

Solution

  1. Identify the knowns. vˉ=4.00 m/s\kvb = 4.00\ \text{m/s}vˉ=4.00 m/s, Δt=2.00 min\kdt = 2.00\ \text{min}Δt=2.00 min, and x0=0 m\kxo = 0\ \text{m}x0​=0 m.
  2. Enter the known values into the equation. x=x0+vˉt=0+(4.00 m/s)(120 s)=480 m\kx = \kxo + \kvb\kt = 0 + (4.00\ \text{m/s})(120\ \text{s}) = 480\ \text{m}x=x0​+vˉt=0+(4.00 m/s)(120 s)=480 m

Discussion

Velocity and final displacement are both positive, which means they are in the same direction.

The equation x=x0+vˉt\kx = \kxo + \kvb\ktx=x0​+vˉt gives insight into the relationship between displacement, average velocity, and time. It shows, for example, that displacement is a linear function of average velocity. (By linear function, we mean that displacement depends on vˉ\kvbvˉ rather than on vˉ\kvbvˉ raised to some other power, such as vˉ2\kvb^2vˉ2. When graphed, linear functions look like straight lines with a constant slope.) On a car trip, for example, we will get twice as far in a given time if we average 90 km/h than if we average 45 km/h.

Final velocity

Solving for Final Velocity

We can derive another useful equation by manipulating the definition of acceleration.

a=ΔvΔt\ka = \frac{\kdv}{\kdt}a=ΔtΔv​

Substituting the simplified notation for Δv\kdvΔv and Δt\kdtΔt gives us

a=v−v0t  (constant a).\ka = \frac{\kv - \kvo}{\kt}\;(\text{constant }\ka).a=tv−v0​​(constant a).

Solving for v\kvv yields

v=v0+at  (constant a).\kv = \kvo + \ka\kt\;(\text{constant }\ka).v=v0​+at(constant a).

Example 2.9 · Calculating Final Velocity: An Airplane Slowing Down after Landing

An airplane lands with an initial velocity of 70.0 m/s and then decelerates at 1.50 m/s21.50\ \text{m/s}^21.50 m/s2 for 40.0 s. What is its final velocity?

Strategy

Draw a sketch. We draw the acceleration vector in the direction opposite the velocity vector because the plane is decelerating.

Solution

  1. Identify the knowns. v0=70.0 m/s\kvo = 70.0\ \text{m/s}v0​=70.0 m/s, a=−1.50 m/s2\ka = -1.50\ \text{m/s}^2a=−1.50 m/s2, t=40.0 s\kt = 40.0\ \text{s}t=40.0 s.
  2. Identify the unknown. In this case, it is final velocity, vfv_fvf​.
  3. Determine which equation to use. We can calculate the final velocity using the equation v=v0+at\kv = \kvo + \ka\ktv=v0​+at.
  4. Plug in the known values and solve. v=v0+at=70.0 m/s+(−1.50 m/s2)(40.0 s)=10.0 m/s\kv = \kvo + \ka\kt = 70.0\ \text{m/s} + (-1.50\ \text{m/s}^2)(40.0\ \text{s}) = 10.0\ \text{m/s}v=v0​+at=70.0 m/s+(−1.50 m/s2)(40.0 s)=10.0 m/s

Discussion

The final velocity is much less than the initial velocity, as desired when slowing down, but still positive. With jet engines, reverse thrust could be maintained long enough to stop the plane and start moving it backward. That would be indicated by a negative final velocity, which is not the case here.

In addition to being useful in problem solving, the equation v=v0+at\kv = \kvo + \ka\ktv=v0​+at gives us insight into the relationships among velocity, acceleration, and time. From it we can see, for example, that

  • final velocity depends on how large the acceleration is and how long it lasts
  • if the acceleration is zero, then the final velocity equals the initial velocity (v=v0)(\kv = \kvo)(v=v0​), as expected (i.e., velocity is constant)
  • if a\kaa is negative, then the final velocity is less than the initial velocity

(All of these observations fit our intuition, and it is always useful to examine basic equations in light of our intuition and experiences to check that they do indeed describe nature accurately.)

Making Connections: Real-World Connection

An intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) has a larger average acceleration than the Space Shuttle and achieves a greater velocity in the first minute or two of flight (actual ICBM burn times are classified—short-burn-time missiles are more difficult for an enemy to destroy). But the Space Shuttle obtains a greater final velocity, so that it can orbit the earth rather than come directly back down as an ICBM does. The Space Shuttle does this by accelerating for a longer time.

Final position when velocity is not constant

Adding v0\kvov0​ to each side of this equation and dividing by 2 gives

Since v0+v2=vˉ\frac{\kvo + \kv}{2} = \kvb2v0​+v​=vˉ for constant acceleration, then

Now we substitute this expression for vˉ\kvbvˉ into the equation for displacement, x=x0+vˉt\kx = \kxo + \kvb\ktx=x0​+vˉt, yielding

Example 2.10 · Calculating Displacement of an Accelerating Object: Dragsters

Dragsters can achieve average accelerations of 26.0 m/s226.0\ \text{m/s}^226.0 m/s2. Suppose such a dragster accelerates from rest at this rate for 5.56 s. How far does it travel in this time?

Strategy

Draw a sketch. We are asked to find displacement, which is x\kxx if we take x0\kxox0​ to be zero. (Think about it like the starting line of a race. It can be anywhere, but we call it 0 and measure all other positions relative to it.) We can use the equation x=x0+v0t+12at2\kx = \kxo + \kvo\kt + \tfrac{1}{2}\ka\kt^2x=x0​+v0​t+21​at2 once we identify v0\kvov0​, a\kaa, and t\ktt from the statement of the problem.

Solution

  1. Identify the knowns. Starting from rest means that v0=0\kvo = 0v0​=0, a\kaa is given as 26.0 m/s226.0\ \text{m/s}^226.0 m/s2 and t\ktt is given as 5.56 s.
  2. Plug the known values into the equation to solve for the unknown x\kxx: x=x0+v0t+12at2.\kx = \kxo + \kvo\kt + \tfrac{1}{2}\ka\kt^2.x=x0​+v0​t+21​at2. Since the initial position and velocity are both zero, this simplifies to x=12at2.\kx = \tfrac{1}{2}\ka\kt^2.x=21​at2. Substituting the identified values of a\kaa and t\ktt gives x=12(26.0 m/s2)(5.56 s)2,\kx = \tfrac{1}{2}(26.0\ \text{m/s}^2)(5.56\ \text{s})^2,x=21​(26.0 m/s2)(5.56 s)2, yielding x=402 m.\kx = 402\ \text{m}.x=402 m.

Discussion

If we convert 402 m to miles, we find that the distance covered is very close to one quarter of a mile, the standard distance for drag racing. So the answer is reasonable. This is an impressive displacement in only 5.56 s, but top-notch dragsters can do a quarter mile in even less time than this.

What else can we learn by examining the equation x=x0+v0t+12at2\kx = \kxo + \kvo\kt + \tfrac{1}{2}\ka\kt^2x=x0​+v0​t+21​at2? We see that:

  • displacement depends on the square of the elapsed time when acceleration is not zero. In Example 2.10, the dragster covers only one fourth of the total distance in the first half of the elapsed time
  • if acceleration is zero, then the initial velocity equals average velocity (v0=vˉ\kvo = \kvbv0​=vˉ) and x=x0+v0t+12at2\kx = \kxo + \kvo\kt + \tfrac{1}{2}\ka\kt^2x=x0​+v0​t+21​at2 becomes x=x0+v0t\kx = \kxo + \kvo\ktx=x0​+v0​t

Example 2.11 · Calculating Final Velocity: Dragsters

Calculate the final velocity of the dragster in Example 2.10 without using information about time.

Strategy

  1. Identify the known values. We know that v0=0\kvo = 0v0​=0, since the dragster starts from rest. 145 m/s is about 522 km/h or about 324 mi/h, but even this breakneck speed is short of the record for the quarter mile. Also, note that a square root has two values; we took the positive value to indicate a velocity in the same direction as the acceleration.

An examination of the equation v2=v02+2a(x−x0)\kv^2 = \kvo^2 + 2\ka(\kx - \kxo)v2=v0​2+2a(x−x0​) can produce further insights into the general relationships among physical quantities:

  • The final velocity depends on how large the acceleration is and the distance over which it acts
  • For a fixed deceleration, a car that is going twice as fast doesn’t simply stop in twice the distance—it takes much further to stop. (This is why we have reduced speed zones near schools.)

Example 2.13 · Calculating Time: A Car Merges into Traffic

Suppose a car merges into freeway traffic on a 200-m-long ramp. If its initial velocity is 10.0 m/s and it accelerates at 2.00 m/s22.00\ \text{m/s}^22.00 m/s2, how long does it take to travel the 200 m up the ramp? (Such information might be useful to a traffic engineer.)

This yields two solutions for t\ktt, which are t=10.0 and−20.0.\kt = 10.0\ \text{and} -20.0.t=10.0 and−20.0. In this case, then, the time is t=t\kt = tt=t in seconds, or t=10.0 s and−20.0 s.\kt = 10.0\ \text{s}\ \text{and} -20.0\ \text{s}.t=10.0 s and−20.0 s. A negative value for time is unreasonable, since it would mean that the event happened 20 s before the motion began. We can discard that solution. Thus, t=10.0 s.\kt = 10.0\ \text{s}.t=10.0 s.

Discussion

Whenever an equation contains an unknown squared, there will be two solutions. In some problems both solutions are meaningful, but in others, such as the above, only one solution is reasonable. The 10.0 s answer seems reasonable for a typical freeway on-ramp.

With the basics of kinematics established, we can go on to many other interesting examples and applications. In the process of developing kinematics, we have also glimpsed a general approach to problem solving that produces both correct answers and insights into physical relationships.
