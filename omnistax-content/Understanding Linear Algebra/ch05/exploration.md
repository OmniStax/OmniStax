## What is new in the chapter

Computers approximate arithmetic: 0.1 has an infinite base-2 expansion, a three-digit computer adds 1.023 + 0.023421 as 1.02 + 0.0234 = 1.04, and the ⅔n³ operations of Gaussian elimination on a 1000 × 1000 matrix let round off error accumulate; the system 0.0001x + y = 1, x + y = 2 solved on a three-digit computer gives x ≈ 0.00 in the printed order and the best possible x ≈ 1.00 after interchanging the equations; the graphical reason, that a small error in y moves x far along a nearly horizontal line and little along a line of slope −1; partial pivoting, the row interchange that puts the entry of largest absolute value into the pivot position, performed at every step in practice (5.1). Solving Ax = b₁ and Ax = b₂ without repeating the elimination: the row replacements of Gaussian elimination as elementary matrices E₃E₂E₁A = U, so A = (E₃E₂E₁)⁻¹U = LU with L lower triangular; solving Ax = L(Ux) = b as Lc = b by forward substitution and Ux = c by back substitution; a triangular solve costs about ½n², the pair n², against ⅔n³ for the elimination, which is paid once and reused for every b; row interchanges break the triangular structure, so software returns PA = LU with P a permutation matrix and solves Lc = Pb, Ux = c; Sage's `P, L, U = A.LU()` uses partial pivoting and finds a different factorization; in the cards, A invertible iff U is, Nul(C) = Nul(U), A⁻¹ = U⁻¹L⁻¹P, det A = ± the product of the pivots, and the ill-conditioned matrix [1 1; 1 1.0001] (5.1). Finding eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial and null spaces is impractical for large matrices, and round off makes A − λI look invertible; the dominant eigenvalue, the one of largest absolute value; x_{k+1} = Ax_k, so x_k = Aᵏx₀ = c₁λ₁ᵏv₁ + c₂λ₂ᵏv₂ draws toward the dominant eigenspace, a Markov chain being the case λ₁ = 1; the power method: scale x_k by the reciprocal of its component m_k of largest absolute value, so x̄_k → v₁ and m_k → λ₁; the initial vector must have some contribution from v₁; the inverse power method finds the eigenvalue of smallest absolute value as the reciprocal of the dominant eigenvalue of A⁻¹, solving Ax_{k+1} = x_k with an LU factorization rather than forming A⁻¹; the shift A − sI has eigenvalues λ − s, so the inverse power method on A − sI finds the eigenvalue of A closest to s, as 1/m_k + s, and probing s between −λ₁ and λ₁ finds the rest; the method assumes real, distinct eigenvalues and fails or needs modification for ±λ pairs, complex eigenvalues, repeated eigenvalues and the sign-flipping case of Exercise 5.2.5 (5.2).
