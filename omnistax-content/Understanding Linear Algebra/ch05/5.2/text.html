Eigenvalues by approximation

We have typically found eigenvalues of a square matrix $\kA$ as the roots of the characteristic polynomial $\det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = 0$ and the associated eigenvectors as the null space $\kNul(\kA-\klambda \kI)$. Unfortunately, this approach is not practical when we are working with large matrices. First, finding the charactertic polynomial of a large matrix requires considerable computation, as does finding the roots of that polynomial. Second, finding the null space of a singular matrix is plagued by numerical problems, as we will see in the preview activity.

For this reason, we will explore a technique called the power method that finds numerical approximations to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a square matrix.

Introduction

The power method

Our goal is to find a technique that produces numerical approximations to the eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors of a matrix $\kA$. We begin by searching for the eigenvalue having the largest absolute value, which is called the dominant eigenvalue. The next two examples demonstrate this technique.

Example 5.2.1

Let’s begin with the positive stochastic matrix $\kA=\left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.7 & 0.6 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 \\ \end{array}\right]$. We spent quite a bit of time studying this type of matrix in Section 4.5; in particular, we saw that any Markov chain will converge to the unique steady-state vector. Let’s rephrase this statement in terms of the eigenvectors of $\kA$.

This matrix has eigenvalues $\klambdaone = 1$ and $\klambdatwo =0.1$ so the dominant eigenvalue is $\klambdaone = 1$. The associated eigenvectors are $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. Suppose we begin with the vector

$$ \kvecxzero = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] = \frac13 \kvecvone - \frac13 \kvecvtwo $$

and find

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecxone & {}={} \kA\kvecxzero = \frac13 \kvecvone - \frac13(0.1) \kvecvtwo \\ \kvecxtwo & {}={} \kA^2\kvecxzero = \frac13 \kvecvone - \frac13(0.1)^2 \kvecvtwo \\ \kvecxthree & {}={} \kA^3\kvecxzero = \frac13 \kvecvone - \frac13(0.1)^3 \kvecvtwo \\ & \vdots \\ \kvecxk & {}={} \kAk\kvecxzero = \frac13 \kvecvone - \frac13(0.1)^k \kvecvtwo \\ \end{aligned} $$

and so forth. Notice that the powers $0.1^k$ become increasingly small as $k$ grows so that $\kvecxk\approx \frac13\kvecvone$ when $k$ is large. Therefore, the vectors $\kvecxk$ become increasingly close to a vector in the eigenspace $\kEone$, the eigenspace associated to the dominant eigenvalue. If we did not know the eigenvector $\kvecvone$, we could use a Markov chain in this way to find a basis vector for $\kEone$, which, as seen in Section 4.5, is essentially how the Google PageRank algorithm works.

Example 5.2.2

Let’s now look at the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ \end{array}\right]$, which has eigenvalues $\klambdaone=3$ and $\klambdatwo = 1$. The dominant eigenvalue is $\klambdaone=3$, and the associated eigenvectors are $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. Once again, begin with the vector $\kvecxzero = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]=\frac12 \kvecvone - \frac12 \kvecvtwo$ so that

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecxone & {}={} \kA\kvecxzero = 3\frac12 \kvecvone - \frac12 \kvecvtwo \\ \kvecxtwo & {}={} \kA^2\kvecxzero = 3^2\frac13 \kvecvone - \frac12 \kvecvtwo \\ \kvecxthree & {}={} \kA^3\kvecxzero = 3^3\frac13 \kvecvone - \frac12 \kvecvtwo \\ & \vdots \\ \kvecxk & {}={} \kAk\kvecxzero = 3^k\frac13 \kvecvone - \frac12 \kvecvtwo\text{.} \\ \end{aligned} $$

As the figure shows, the vectors $\kvecxk$ are stretched by a factor of $3$ in the $\kvecvone$ direction and not at all in the $\kvecvtwo$ direction. Consequently, the vectors $\kvecxk$ become increasingly long, but their direction becomes closer to the direction of the eigenvector $\kvecvone=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ associated to the dominant eigenvalue.

To find an eigenvector associated to the dominant eigenvalue, we will prevent the length of the vectors $\kvecxk$ from growing arbitrarily large by multiplying by an appropriate scaling constant. Here is one way to do this. Given the vector $\kvecxk$, we identify its component having the largest absolute value and call it $m_k$. We then define $\kvecxkbar = \frac{1}{m_k} \kvecxk$, which means that the component of $\kvecxkbar$ having the largest absolute value is $1$.

For example, beginning with $\kvecxzero = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$, we find $\kvecxone = \kA\kvecxzero = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. The component of $\kvecxone$ having the largest absolute value is $m_1=2$ so we multiply by $\frac{1}{m_1} = \frac12$ to obtain $\kvecxonebar = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ \frac12 \end{array}\right]$. Then $\kvecxtwo = \kA\kvecxonebar = \left[\begin{array}{r}\frac52 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]$. Now the component having the largest absolute value is $m_2=\frac52$ so we multiply by $\frac25$ to obtain $\kvecxtwobar = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ \frac45 \end{array}\right]$.

The resulting sequence of vectors $\kvecxkbar$ is shown in the figure. Notice how the vectors $\kvecxkbar$ now approach the eigenvector $\kvecvone$, which gives us a way to find the eigenvector $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. This is the power method for finding an eigenvector associated to the dominant eigenvalue of a matrix.

Notice that the power method gives us not only an eigenvector $\kvecv$ but also its associated eigenvalue. As in the activity, consider the matrix $\kA=\left[\begin{array}{rr} -5.1 & 5.7 \\ -3.8 & 4.4 \\ \end{array}\right]$, which has eigenvector $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}3 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]$. The first component has the largest absolute value so we multiply by $\frac13$ to obtain $\kvecvbar=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ \frac23 \end{array}\right]$. When we multiply by $\kA$, we have $\kA\kvecvbar = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1.30 \\ -0.86 \end{array}\right]$. Notice that the first component still has the largest absolute value so that the multiplier $m=-1.3$ is the eigenvalue $\klambda$ corresponding to the eigenvector. This demonstrates the fact that the multipliers $m_k$ approach the eigenvalue $\klambda$ having the largest absolute value.

Notice that the power method requires us to choose an initial vector $\kvecxzero$. For most choices, this method will find the eigenvalue having the largest absolute value. However, an unfortunate choice of $\kvecxzero$ may not. For instance, if we had chosen $\kvecxzero = {}$$\kvecvtwo$ in our example above, the vectors in the sequence $\kvecxk = \kAk\kvecxzero=\klambdatwo^k$$\kvecvtwo$ will not detect the eigenvector $\kvecvone$. However, it usually happens that our initial guess $\kvecxzero$ has some contribution from $\kvecvone$ that enables us to find it.

The power method, as presented here, will fail for certain unlucky matrices. This is examined in Exercise 5.2.5 along with a means to improve the power method to work for all matrices.

Finding other eigenvalues

The power method gives a technique for finding the dominant eigenvalue of a matrix. We can modify the method to find the other eigenvalues as well.

With the power method and the inverse power method, we can now find the eigenvalues of a matrix $\kA$ having the largest and smallest absolute values. With one more modification, we can find all the eigenvalues of $\kA$.

There are some restrictions on the matrices to which this technique applies as we have assumed that the eigenvalues of $\kA$ are real and distinct. If $\kA$ has repeated or complex eigenvalues, this technique will need to be modified, as explored in some of the exercises.

def power(A, x, N):
    for i in range(N):
        x = A*x
        m = max([comp for comp in x], key=abs).numerical_approx(digits=14)
        x = 1/float(m)*x
        print (m, x)
def find_closest_eigenvalue(A, s, x, N):
    B = A-s*identity_matrix(A.nrows())
    for i in range(N):
        x = B \ x
        m = max([comp for comp in x], key=abs).numerical_approx(digits=14)
        x = 1/float(m)*x
        print (1/float(m)+s, x)
def inverse_power(A, x, N):
    find_closest_eigenvalue(A, 0, x, N)
