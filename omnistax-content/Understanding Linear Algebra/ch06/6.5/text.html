Orthogonality and data

Our work so far has developed a rich set of tools that allow us to use orthogonality to simplify some problems. As we will see in this section, these tools lead naturally to several important techniques for working with data, such as linear regression.

Introduction

Suppose we collect some data when performing an experiment and plot it as shown on the left of Figure 6.5.1. Notice that there is no line on which all the points lie; in fact, it would be surprising if there were since we can expect some uncertainty in the measurements recorded. There does, however, appear to be a line, as shown on the right, on which the points almost lie.

In this section, we'll explore how the techniques developed in this chapter enable us to find the line that best approximates the data. More specifically, we'll see how the search for a line passing through the data points leads to an inconsistent system $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$. Since we are unable to find a solution, we instead seek the vector $\kvecx$ where $\kA\kvecx$ is as close as possible to $\kvecb$. Orthogonal projection gives us just the right tool for doing this.

A first example

When we've encountered inconsistent systems in the past, we've simply said there is no solution and moved on. The preview activity, however, shows how we can find approximate solutions to an inconsistent system: if there are no solutions to $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$, we instead solve the consistent system $\kA\kvecx = \kvecbhat$, the orthogonal projection of $\kvecb$ onto $\kCol(\kA)$. As we'll see, this solution is, in a specific sense, the best possible.

This activity illustrates the idea behind a technique known as orthogonal least squares, which we have been working toward throughout this chapter. If the data points are denoted as $(x_i, y_i)$, we construct the matrix $\kA$ and vector $\kvecb$ as

$$ \kA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ 1 & x_2 \\ 1 & x_3 \\ \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kvecb = \left[\begin{array}{r}y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

With the vector $\kvecx=\left[\begin{array}{c}b \\ m \end{array}\right]$ representing the line $b+mx = y$, we see that the equation $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ describes a line passing through all the data points. In our activity, it is visually apparent that there is no such line, which agrees with the fact that the equation $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ is inconsistent.

Remember that $\kvecbhat$, the orthogonal projection of $\kvecb$ onto $\kCol(\kA)$, is the closest vector in $\kCol(\kA)$ to $\kvecb$. Therefore, when we solve the equation $\kA\kvecx=\kvecbhat$, we are finding the vector $\kvecx$ so that $\kA\kvecx = \left[\begin{array}{r}b+mx_1 \\ b+mx_2 \\ b+mx_3 \end{array}\right]$ is as close to $\kvecb=\left[\begin{array}{r}y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{array}\right]$ as possible. Let's think about what this means within the context of this problem.

The difference $\kvecb-\kA\kvecx = \left[\begin{array}{r}y_1-(b+mx_1) \\ y_2-(b+mx_2) \\ y_3-(b+mx_3) \end{array}\right]$ so that the square of the distance between $\kA\kvecx$ and $\kvecb$ is

$$ \begin{aligned} \left|\kvecb - \kA\kvecx\right|^2 & = \\ & \left(y_1-(b+mx_1)\right)^2 + \left(y_2-(b+mx_2)\right)^2 + \left(y_3-(b+mx_3)\right)^2\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

Our approach finds the values for $b$ and $m$ that make this sum of squares as small as possible, which is why we call this a least-squares problem.

Drawing the line defined by the vector $\kvecx=\left[\begin{array}{c}b \\ m \end{array}\right]$, the quantity $y_i - (b + mx_i)$ reflects the vertical distance between the line and the data point $(x_i, y_i)$, as shown in Figure 6.5.3. Seen in this way, the square of the distance $\left|\kvecb-\kA\kvecx\right|^2$ is a measure of how much the line defined by the vector $\kvecx$ misses the data points. The solution to the least-squares problem is the line that misses the data points by the smallest amount possible.

Solving least-squares problems

Now that we've seen an example of what we're trying to accomplish, let's put this technique into a more general framework.

Given an inconsistent system $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$, we seek the vector $\kvecx$ that minimizes the distance from $\kA\kvecx$ to $\kvecb$. In other words, $\kvecx$ satisfies $\kA\kvecx = \kvecbhat$, where $\kvecbhat$ is the orthogonal projection of $\kvecb$ onto the column space $\kCol(\kA)$. We know the equation $\kA\kvecx=\kvecbhat$ is consistent since $\kvecbhat$ is in $\kCol(\kA)$, and we know there is only one solution if we assume that the columns of $\kA$ are linearly independent.

We will usually denote the solution of $\kA\kvecx = \kvecbhat$ by $\kvecxhat$ and call this vector the least-squares approximate solution of $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ to distinguish it from a (possibly non-existent) solution of $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$.

There is an alternative method for finding $\kvecxhat$ that does not involve first finding the orthogonal projection $\kvecbhat$. Remember that $\kvecbhat$ is defined by the fact that $\kvecbhat - \kvecb$ is orthogonal to $\kCol(\kA)$. In other words, $\kvecbhat-\kvecb$ is in the orthogonal complement $\kCol(\kA)^\perp$, which Proposition 6.2.10 tells us is the same as $\kNul(\kAT)$. Since $\kvecbhat-\kvecb$ is in $\kNul(\kAT)$, it follows that

$$ \kAT(\kvecbhat-\kvecb) = \kveczero\text{.} $$

Because the least-squares approximate solution is the vector $\kvecxhat$ such that $\kA\kvecxhat = \kvecbhat$, we can rearrange this equation to see that

This equation is called the normal equation, and we have the following proposition.

Proposition 6.5.4

If the columns of $\kA$ are linearly independent, then there is a unique least-squares approximate solution $\kvecxhat$ to the equation $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ given by the normal equation

$$ \kAT\kA\kvecxhat = \kAT\kvecb\text{.} $$

Example 6.5.5

Consider the equation

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 \\ \end{bmatrix} \kvecx = \left[\begin{array}{r}16 \\ -1 \\ 7 \end{array}\right] $$

with matrix $\kA$ and vector $\kvecb$. Since this equation is inconsistent, we will find the least-squares approximate solution $\kvecxhat$ by solving the normal equation $\kAT\kA\kvecxhat = \kAT\kvecb$, which has the form

$$ \kAT\kA\kvecxhat = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & -1 \\ -1 & 10 \\ \end{bmatrix} \kvecxhat = \left[\begin{array}{r}23 \\ 37 \end{array}\right] = \kAT\kvecb $$

and the solution $\kvecxhat=\left[\begin{array}{r}3 \\ 4 \end{array}\right]$.

This example demonstrates an approach, called linear regression, in which a collection of data is modeled using a linear function found by solving a least-squares problem. Once we have the linear function that best fits the data, we can make predictions about situations that we haven't encountered in the data.

If we're going to use our function to make predictions, it's natural to ask how much confidence we have in these predictions. This is a statistical question that leads to a rich and well-developed theory, which we won't explore in much detail here. However, there is one simple measure of how well our linear function fits the data that is known as the coefficient of determination and denoted by $R^2$.

We have seen that the square of the distance $\left|\kvecb-\kA\kvecx\right|^2$ measures the amount by which the line fails to pass through the data points. When the line is close to the data points, we expect this number to be small. However, the size of this measure depends on the scale of the data. For instance, the two lines shown in Figure 6.5.6 seem to fit the data equally well, but $|\kvecb-\kA\kvecxhat|^2$ is 100 times larger on the right.

The coefficient of determination $R^2$ is defined by normalizing $|\kvecb-\kA\kvecxhat|^2$ so that it is independent of the scale. Recall that we described how to demean a vector in Section 6.1: given a vector $\mathbf{v}$, we obtain $\kvecvtilde$ by subtracting the average of the components from each component.

Definition 6.5.7 · Coefficient of determination

The coefficient of determination is

$$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{|\kvecb - \kA\kvecxhat|^2} {|\kvecbtilde|^2}, $$

where $\kvecbtilde$ is the vector obtained by demeaning $\kvecb$.

A more complete explanation of this definition relies on the concept of variance, which we explore in Exercise 6.5.12 and the next chapter. For the time being, it's enough to know that $0\leq R^2 \leq 1$ and that the closer $R^2$ is to 1, the better the line fits the data. In our original example, illustrated in Figure 6.5.6, we find that $R^2 = 0.75$, and in our study of cricket chirp rates, we have $R^2=0.69$. However, assessing the confidence we have in predictions made by solving a least-squares problem can require considerable thought, and it would be naive to rely only on the value of $R^2$.

Using $\kQ\kR$ factorizations

As we've seen, the least-squares approximate solution $\kvecxhat$ to $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ may be found by solving the normal equation $\kAT\kA\kvecxhat = \kAT\kvecb$, and this can be a practical strategy for some problems. However, this approach can be problematic as small rounding errors can accumulate and lead to inaccurate final results.

As the next activity demonstrates, there is an alternate method for finding the least-squares approximate solution $\kvecxhat$ using a $\kQ\kR$ factorization of the matrix $\kA$, and this method is preferable as it is numerically more reliable.

To summarize, we have seen that

Proposition 6.5.8

If the columns of $\kA$ are linearly independent and we have the $\kQ\kR$ factorization $\kA=\kQ\kR$, then the least-squares approximate solution $\kvecxhat$ to the equation $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ is given by

$$ \kvecxhat = \kRinv\kQT\kvecb\text{.} $$

Polynomial Regression

In the examples we've seen so far, we have fit a linear function to a dataset. Sometimes, however, a polynomial, such as a quadratic function, may be more appropriate. It turns out that the techniques we've developed in this section are still useful as the next activity demonstrates.

The matrices $\kA$ that you created in the last activity when fitting a quadratic and cubic function to a dataset have a special form. In particular, if the data points are labeled $(x_i, y_i)$ and we seek a degree $k$ polynomial, then

$$ \kA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \ldots & x_1^k \\ 1 & x_2 & x_2^2 & \ldots & x_2^k \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_m & x_m^2 & \ldots & x_m^k \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

This is called a Vandermonde matrix of degree $k$.
