Orthogonal bases from any basis

The last section demonstrated the value of working with orthogonal, and especially orthonormal, sets. If we have an orthogonal basis $\kvecwone,\kvecwtwo,\ldots,\kvecwn$ for a subspace $\kW$, the Projection Formula tells us that the orthogonal projection of a vector $\kvecb$ onto $\kW$ is

$$ \kvecbhat = \frac{\kvecb\cdot\kvecwone}{\kvecwone\cdot\kvecwone}~\kvecwone + \frac{\kvecb\cdot\kvecwtwo}{\kvecwtwo\cdot\kvecwtwo}~\kvecwtwo + \cdots + \frac{\kvecb\cdot\kvecwn}{\kvecwn\cdot\kvecwn}~\kvecwn\text{.} $$

An orthonormal basis $\kvecuone,\kvecutwo,\ldots,\kvecun$ is even more convenient: after forming the matrix $\kQ=\begin{bmatrix} \kvecuone & \kvecutwo & \ldots & \kvecun \end{bmatrix}$, we have $\kvecbhat = \kQ\kQT\kvecb$.

In the examples we’ve seen so far, however, orthogonal bases were given to us. What we need now is a way to form orthogonal bases. In this section, we’ll explore an algorithm that begins with a basis for a subspace and creates an orthogonal basis. Once we have an orthogonal basis, we can scale each of the vectors appropriately to produce an orthonormal basis.

Introduction

Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization

The preview activity illustrates the main idea behind an algorithm, known as Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, that begins with a basis for some subspace of ${\mathbb R}^m$ and produces an orthogonal or orthonormal basis. The algorithm relies on our construction of the orthogonal projection. Remember that we formed the orthogonal projection $\kvecbhat$ of $\kvecb$ onto a subspace $\kW$ by requiring that $\kvecb-\kvecbhat$ is orthogonal to $\kW$ as shown in Figure 6.4.3.

This observation guides our construction of an orthogonal basis for it allows us to create a vector that is orthogonal to a given subspace. Let’s see how the Gram-Schmidt algorithm works.

As this activity illustrates, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization begins with a basis $\kvecvone\kvecvtwo,\ldots,\kvecvn$ for a subspace $\kW$ of ${\mathbb R}^m$ and creates an orthogonal basis for $\kW$. Let’s work through a second example.

Example 6.4.4

Let’s start with the basis

$$ \kvecvone=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right],\hspace{24pt} \kvecvtwo=\left[\begin{array}{r}-3 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{array}\right],\hspace{24pt} \kvecvthree=\left[\begin{array}{r}-2 \\ 7 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]\text{,} $$

which is a basis for ${\mathbb R}^3$.

To get started, we’ll simply set $\kvecwone=\kvecvone=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]$. We construct $\kvecwtwo$ from $\kvecvtwo$ by subtracting its orthogonal projection onto $\kWone$, the line defined by $\kvecwone$. This gives

$$ \kvecwtwo = \kvecvtwo - \frac{\kvecvtwo\cdot\kvecwone}{\kvecwone\cdot\kvecwone}\kvecwone = \kvecvtwo + \kvecwone = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

Notice that we found $\kvecvtwo = -\kvecwone + \kvecwtwo$. Therefore, we can rewrite any linear combination of $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ as

$$ c_1\kvecvone + c_2\kvecvtwo = c_1\kvecwone + c_2(-\kvecwone+\kvecwtwo) = (c_1-c_2)\kvecwone + c_2\kvecwtwo\text{,} $$

a linear combination of $\kvecwone$ and $\kvecwtwo$. This tells us that

$$ \kWtwo = \kSpan\left\{\kvecwone,\kvecwtwo\right\} = \kSpan\left\{\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo\right\}\text{.} $$

In other words, $\kvecwone$ and $\kvecwtwo$ is a orthogonal basis for $\kWtwo$, the 2-dimensional subspace that is the span of $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$.

Finally, we form $\kvecwthree$ from $\kvecvthree$ by subtracting its orthogonal projection onto $\kWtwo$:

$$ \kvecwthree = \kvecvthree - \frac{\kvecvthree\cdot\kvecwone}{\kvecwone\cdot\kvecwone}\kvecwone - \frac{\kvecvthree\cdot\kvecwtwo}{\kvecwtwo\cdot\kvecwtwo}\kvecwtwo = \kvecvthree + \kvecwone - 2\kvecwtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

We can now check that

$$ \kvecwone=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right],\hspace{24pt} \kvecwtwo=\left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{array}\right],\hspace{24pt} \kvecwthree=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 2 \\ -1 \end{array}\right],\hspace{24pt} $$

is an orthogonal set. Furthermore, we have, as before, $\kSpan\left\{\kvecwone,\kvecwtwo,\kvecwthree\right\} = \kSpan\left\{\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo,\kvecvthree\right\}$, which says that we have found a new orthogonal basis for ${\mathbb R}^3$.

To create an orthonormal basis, we form unit vectors parallel to each of the vectors in the orthogonal basis:

$$ \kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}2/3 \\ -1/3 \\ 2/3 \end{array}\right],\hspace{24pt} \kvecutwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1/3 \\ 2/3 \\ 2/3 \end{array}\right],\hspace{24pt} \kvecuthree = \left[\begin{array}{r}2/3 \\ 2/3 \\ -1/3 \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

More generally, if we have a basis $\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo,\ldots,\kvecvn$ for a subspace $\kW$ of ${\mathbb R}^m$, the Gram-Schmidt algorithm creates an orthogonal basis for $\kW$ in the following way:

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecwone & = \kvecvone \\ \kvecwtwo & = \kvecvtwo - \frac{\kvecvtwo\cdot\kvecwone}{\kvecwone\cdot\kvecwone}\kvecwone \\ \kvecwthree & = \kvecvthree - \frac{\kvecvthree\cdot\kvecwone}{\kvecwone\cdot\kvecwone}\kvecwone - \frac{\kvecvthree\cdot\kvecwtwo}{\kvecwtwo\cdot\kvecwtwo}\kvecwtwo \\ & \vdots \\ \kvecwn & = \kvecvn - \frac{\kvecvn\cdot\kvecwone}{\kvecwone\cdot\kvecwone}\kvecwone - \frac{\kvecvn\cdot\kvecwtwo}{\kvecwtwo\cdot\kvecwtwo}\kvecwtwo - \ldots - \frac{\kvecvn\cdot\kvecwnmone} {\kvecwnmone\cdot\kvecwnmone}\kvecwnmone \text{.} \end{aligned} $$

From here, we may form an orthonormal basis by constructing a unit vector parallel to each vector in the orthogonal basis: $\kvecuj = 1/\left|\kvecwj\right|~\kvecwj$.

$QR$ factorizations

Now that we’ve seen how the Gram-Schmidt algorithm forms an orthonormal basis for a given subspace, we will explore how the algorithm leads to an important matrix factorization known as the $QR$ factorization.

When the columns of a matrix $\kA$ are linearly independent, they form a basis for $\kCol(\kA)$ so that we can perform the Gram-Schmidt algorithm. The previous activity shows how this leads to a factorization of $\kA$ as the product of a matrix $\kQ$ whose columns are an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kA)$ and an upper triangular matrix $\kR$.

Proposition 6.4.5 · QR factorization

If $\kA$ is an $m\times n$ matrix whose columns are linearly independent, we may write $\kA=\kQ\kR$ where $\kQ$ is an $m\times n$ matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kA)$ and $\kR$ is an $n\times n$ upper triangular matrix.

Example 6.4.6

We’ll consider the matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -3 & -2 \\ -1 & 3 & 7 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ whose columns, which we’ll denote $\kvecvone$, $\kvecvtwo$, and $\kvecvthree$, are the basis of ${\mathbb R}^3$ that we considered in Example 6.4.4. There we found an orthogonal basis $\kvecwone$, $\kvecwtwo$, and $\kvecwthree$ that satisfied

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecvone & {}={} \kvecwone \\ \kvecvtwo & {}={} -\kvecwone + \kvecwtwo \\ \kvecvthree & {}={} -\kvecwone + 2\kvecwtwo + \kvecwthree\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

In terms of the resulting orthonormal basis $\kvecuone$, $\kvecutwo$, and $\kvecuthree$, we had

$$ \kvecwone = 3 \kvecuone,\hspace{24pt} \kvecwtwo = 3 \kvecutwo,\hspace{24pt} \kvecwthree = 3 \kvecuthree $$

so that

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecvone & {}={} 3\kvecuone \\ \kvecvtwo & {}={} -3\kvecuone + 3\kvecutwo \\ \kvecvthree & {}={} -3\kvecuone + 6\kvecutwo + 3\kvecuthree\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

Therefore, if $\kQ=\begin{bmatrix} \kvecuone & \kvecutwo & \kvecuthree \end{bmatrix}$, we have the $QR$ factorization

$$ \kA = \kQ\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -3 & -3 \\ 0 & 3 & 6 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ \end{bmatrix} =\kQ\kR\text{.} $$

The value of the $QR$ factorization will become clear in the next section where we use it to solve least-squares problems.

In fact, Sage provides its own version of the $QR$ factorization that is a bit different than the way we’ve developed the factorization here. For this reason, we have provided our own version of the factorization.
