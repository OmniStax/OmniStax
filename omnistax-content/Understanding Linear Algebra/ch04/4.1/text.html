Vectors that a matrix only stretches

This section introduces the concept of eigenvalues and eigenvectors and offers an example that motivates our interest in them. The point here is to develop an intuitive understanding of eigenvalues and eigenvectors and explain how they can be used to simplify some problems that we have previously encountered. In the rest of this chapter, we will develop this concept into a richer theory and illustrate its use with more meaningful examples.

Introduction

A few examples

We will now introduce the definition of eigenvalues and eigenvectors and then look at a few simple examples.

Definition 4.1.1

Given a square $n\times n$ matrix $\kA$, we say that a nonzero vector $\kvecv$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ if there is a scalar $\klambda$ such that

$$ \kA\kvecv = \klambda \kvecv. $$

The scalar $\klambda$ is called the eigenvalue associated to the eigenvector $\kvecv$.

At first glance, there is a lot going on in this definition so let’s look at an example.

Example 4.1.2

Consider the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 7 & 6 \\ 6 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right]$ and the vector $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. We find that

$$ \kA\kvecv = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 7 & 6 \\ 6 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{r}20 \\ 10 \end{array}\right] =10\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] =10\kvecv. $$

In other words, $\kA\kvecv = 10\kvecv$, which says that $\kvecv$ is an eigenvector of the matrix $\kA$ with associated eigenvalue $\klambda = 10$.

Similarly, if $\kvecw = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]$, we find that

$$ \kA\kvecw = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 7 & 6 \\ 6 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{r}5 \\ -10 \end{array}\right] =-5\left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] =-5\kvecw. $$

Here again, we have $\kA\kvecw = -5\kvecw$ showing that $\kvecw$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with associated eigenvalue $\klambda=-5$.

Let’s consider the ideas we saw in the activity in some more depth. To be an eigenvector of $\kA$, the vector $\kvecv$ must satisfy $\kA\kvecv = \klambda\kvecv$ for some scalar $\klambda$. This means that $\kvecv$ and $\kA\kvecv$ are scalar multiples of each other so they must lie on the same line.

Consider now the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right]$. On the left of Figure 4.1.4, we see that $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$ is not an eigenvector of $\kA$ since the vectors $\kvecv$ and $\kA\kvecv$ do not lie on the same line. On the right, however, we see that $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ is an eigenvector. In fact, $\kA\kvecv$ is obtained from $\kvecv$ by stretching $\kvecv$ by a factor of $3$. Therefore, $\kvecv$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with eigenvalue $\klambda = 3$.

It is not difficult to see that any multiple of $\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ is also an eigenvector of $\kA$ with eigenvalue $\klambda = 3$. Indeed, we will see later that all the eigenvectors associated to a given eigenvalue form a subspace of ${\mathbb R}^n$.

In Figure 4.1.5, we see that $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ is also an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\klambda =-1$.

The interactive diagram we used in the activity is meant to convey the fact that the eigenvectors of a matrix $\kA$ are special vectors. Most of the time, the vectors $\kvecv$ and $\kA\kvecv$ appear visually unrelated. For certain vectors, however, $\kvecv$ and $\kA\kvecv$ line up with one another. Something important is going on when that happens so we call attention to these vectors by calling them eigenvectors. For these vectors, the operation of multiplying by $\kA$ reduces to the much simpler operation of scalar multiplying by $\klambda$. The reason eigenvectors are important is because it is extremely convenient to be able to replace matrix multiplication by scalar multiplication.

The usefulness of eigenvalues and eigenvectors

In the next section, we will introduce an algebraic technique for finding the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix. Before doing that, however, we would like to discuss why eigenvalues and eigenvectors are so useful.

Let’s continue looking at the example $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right]$. We have seen that $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\klambda=3$ and $\kvecvtwo=\left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\klambda = -1$. This means that $\kA\kvecvone = 3\kvecvone$ and $\kA\kvecvtwo=-\kvecvtwo$. By the linearity of matrix multiplication, we can determine what happens when we multiply a linear combination of $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ by $\kA$:

$$ \kA(c_1\kvecvone + c_2\kvecvtwo) = 3c_1\kvecvone - c_2\kvecvtwo. $$

For instance, if we consider the vector $\kvecx=\kvecvone-2\kvecvtwo$, we find that

$$ \begin{aligned} \kA\kvecx&{}={}\kA(\kvecvone - 2\kvecvtwo) \\ \kA\kvecx&{}={} 3\kvecvone + 2\kvecvtwo \end{aligned} $$

as seen in the figure.

In other words, multiplying by $\kA$ has the effect of stretching a vector $\kvecx$ in the $\kvecvone$ direction by a factor of $3$ and flipping $\kvecx$ in $\kvecvtwo$ direction.

We can draw an analogy with the more familiar example of the diagonal matrix $\kD=\left[\begin{array}{rr} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ \end{array}\right]$. As we have seen, the matrix transformation defined by $\kD$ combines a horizontal stretching by a factor of 3 with a reflection across the horizontal axis, as is illustrated in Figure 4.1.6.

The matrix $\kA=\left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right]$ has a similar effect when viewed in the basis defined by the eigenvectors $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$, as seen in Figure 4.1.7.

In a sense that will be made precise later, having a set of eigenvectors of $\kA$ that forms a basis of ${\mathbb R}^2$ enables us to think of $\kA$ as being equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\kD$. Of course, as the other examples in the previous activity show, it may not always be possible to form a basis from the eigenvectors of a matrix. For example, the only eigenvectors of the matrix $\left[\begin{array}{rr} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{array}\right]$, which represents a shear, have the form $\left[\begin{array}{r}x \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$. In this example, we are not able to create a basis for ${\mathbb R}^2$ consisting of eigenvectors of the matrix. This is also true for the matrix $\left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right]$, which represents a $90^\circ$ rotation.

This activity is important and motivates much of our work with eigenvalues and eigenvectors so it’s worth reviewing to make sure we have a clear understanding of the concepts.

First, we compute

$$ \begin{aligned} \kA\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 \\ \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{r}3 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] & {}={} \left[\begin{array}{r}3 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] = 1\kvecvone \\ \\ \kA\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 \\ \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] & {}={} \left[\begin{array}{r}-0.2 \\ 0.2 \end{array}\right] = 0.2\kvecvtwo\text{.} \\ \end{aligned} $$

This shows that $\kvecvone$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with eigenvalue $\klambdaone = 1$ and $\kvecvtwo$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with eigenvalue $\klambdatwo=0.2$.

By the linearity of matrix matrix multiplication, we have

$$ \kA(c_1\kvecvone + c_2\kvecvtwo) = c_1\kvecvone + 0.2c_2\kvecvtwo. $$

Therefore, we will write the vector describing the initial distribution of cars $\kvecxzero=\left[\begin{array}{c}1000 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$ as a linear combination of $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$; that is, $\kvecxzero = c_1\kvecvtwo + c_2 \kvecvtwo$. To do, we form the augmented matrix and row reduce:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo & \kvecxzero \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 3 & -1 & 1000 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 0 & 250 \\ 0 & 1 & -250 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

Therefore, $\kvecxzero = 250\kvecvone -250\kvecvtwo$.

To determine the distribution of cars on subsequent days, we will repeatedly multiply by $\kA$. We find that

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecx_1 = \kA\kvecxzero & {}={} \kA(250\kvecvone - 250\kvecvtwo) = 250\kvecvone - (0.2)250\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecx_2 = \kA\kvecx_1 & {}={} \kA(250\kvecvone - (0.2)250\kvecvtwo) = 250\kvecvone - (0.2)^2250\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecx_3 = \kA\kvecx_2 & {}={} \kA(250\kvecvone - (0.2)^2250\kvecvtwo) = 250\kvecvone - (0.2)^3250\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecx_4 = \kA\kvecx_3 & {}={} \kA(250\kvecvone - (0.2)^3250\kvecvtwo) = 250\kvecvone - (0.2)^4250\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecx_5 = \kA\kvecx_4 & {}={} \kA(250\kvecvone - (0.2)^4250\kvecvtwo) = 250\kvecvone - (0.2)^5250\kvecvtwo. \\ \end{aligned} $$

In particular, this shows us that

$$ \kvecx_5 = 250\kvecvone - (0.2)^5250\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}250\cdot 3 - (0.2)^5250\cdot(-1) \\ 250\cdot 1 - (0.2)^5250\cdot 1 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{r}750.09 \\ 249.92 \end{array}\right]. $$

Taking notice of the pattern, we may write

$$ \kvecxk = 250\kvecvone - (0.2)^k250\kvecvtwo. $$

Multiplying a number by $0.2$ is the same as taking 20% of that number. As each day goes by, the second term is multiplied by $0.2$ so the coefficient of $\kvecvtwo$ in the expression for $\kvecxk$ will eventually become extremely small. We therefore see that the distribution of cars will stabilize at $\kvecx=250\kvecvone=\left[\begin{array}{r}750 \\ 250 \end{array}\right]$.

Notice how our understanding of the eigenvectors of the matrix allows us to replace matrix multiplication with the simpler operation of scalar multiplication. As a result, we can look far into the future without having to repeatedly perform matrix multiplication.

Furthermore, notice how this example relies on the fact that we can express the initial vector $\kvecxzero$ as a linear combination of eigenvectors. For this reason, we would like, when given an $n\times n$ matrix, to be able to create a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ that consists of its eigenvectors. We will frequently return to this question in later sections.
