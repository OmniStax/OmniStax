State vectors, revisited

The last section demonstrated ways in which we may relate a matrix, and the effect that multiplication has on vectors, to a simpler form. For instance, if there is a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$, we saw that $\kA$ is similar to a diagonal matrix $\kD$. As a result, the effect of multiplying vectors by $\kA$, when expressed using the basis of eigenvectors, is the same as multiplying by $\kD$.

In this section, we will put these ideas to use as we explore discrete dynamical systems, first encountered in Subsection 2.5.4. Recall that we used a state vector $\kvecx$ to characterize the state of some system at a particular time, such as the distribution of delivery trucks between two locations. A matrix $\kA$ described the transition of the state vector with $\kA\kvecx$ characterizing the state of the system at a later time. Since we would like to understand how the state vector evolves over time, we are interested in studying the sequence of vectors $\kAk\kvecx$.

Our goal in this section is to describe the types of behaviors that dynamical systems exhibit and to develop a means of detecting these behaviors.

Introduction

A first example

We will begin with a dynamical system that illustrates how the ideas we’ve been developing can help us understand the populations of two interacting species. There are several possible ways in which two species may interact. For example, wolves on Isle Royale in northern Michigan prey on moose so this interaction is often called a predator-prey relationship. Other interactions between species, such as bees and flowering plants, are mutually beneficial for both species.

This activity demonstrates the type of systems we will be considering. In particular, we will have vectors $\kvecxk$ that describe the state of the system at time $k$ and a matrix $\kA$ that describes how the state evolves from one time to the next: $\kvecx_{k+1} = \kA\kvecxk$. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\kA$ provide the key that helps us understand how the vectors $\kvecxk$ evolve and that enables us to make long-range predictions.

Let’s look at the specific example in the previous activity more carefully. We see that

$$ \kvecx_{k+1} = \kA\kvecxk = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.9 & 0.8 \\ 0.2 & 0.9 \\ \end{array}\right] \kvecxk $$

and that the matrix $\kA$ has eigenvectors $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kvecvtwo=\left[\begin{array}{r}-2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ with associated eigenvalues $\klambdaone=1.3$ and $\klambdatwo=0.5$.

With initial populations $\kvecxzero=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 3 \end{array}\right]$, we have

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecxzero & {}={} 2\kvecvone + 1\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecx_1 & {}={} 1.3\cdot2\kvecvone + 0.5\cdot1\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecx_2 & {}={} 1.3^2\cdot2\kvecvone + 0.5^2\cdot1\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecxk & {}={} 1.3^k\cdot2\kvecvone + 0.5^k\cdot1\kvecvtwo. \\ \end{aligned} $$

Let’s shift our perspective slightly. The eigenvectors $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ form a basis ${\cal B}$ of ${\mathbb R}^2$, which says that $\kA$ is diagonalizable; that is, $\kA = \kP\kD\kPinv$ where

$$ \kP = \left[\begin{array}{rr} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 2 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right], \qquad \kD = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1.3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

The coordinate system defined by the basis ${\cal B}$ can be used to express the state vectors. For instance, we can write the initial state vector $\kvecxzero=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 3 \end{array}\right]= 2\kvecvone+\kvecvtwo$, which means that $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_0\right\}_{{\cal B}}} = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. Moreover, $\kvecx_1=\kA\kvecxzero = (1.3)\cdot2\kvecvone + (0.5)\cdot1\kvecvtwo$ so that

$$ \htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_1\right\}_{{\cal B}}} = \left[\begin{array}{r}1.3\cdot 2 \\ 0.5\cdot 1 \end{array}\right] = \kD\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] = \kD\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_0\right\}_{{\cal B}}}. $$

In the same way,

$$ \begin{aligned} \htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_1\right\}_{{\cal B}}} & {}={} \kD\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_0\right\}_{{\cal B}}} = \left[\begin{array}{c}1.3\cdot 2 \\ 0.5\cdot 1 \end{array}\right] \\ \htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_2\right\}_{{\cal B}}} & {}={} \kD\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_1\right\}_{{\cal B}}} = \left[\begin{array}{c}1.3^2\cdot 2 \\ 0.5^2\cdot1 \end{array}\right] \\ \htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_3\right\}_{{\cal B}}} & {}={} \kD\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_2\right\}_{{\cal B}}} = \left[\begin{array}{c}1.3^3\cdot2 \\ 0.5^3\cdot1 \end{array}\right] \\ \htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_k\right\}_{{\cal B}}} & {}={} \left[\begin{array}{c}1.3^k\cdot2 \\ 0.5^k\cdot1 \end{array}\right]\text{.} \\ \end{aligned} $$

More generally, we have

$$ \htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{A\mathbf{x}\right\}_{{\cal B}}} = \kD\kvecxB, $$

which is a restatement of the fact that $\kA$ is similar to $\kD$.

Thinking about this geometrically, we begin with the vector $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_0\right\}_{{\cal B}}}=\left[\begin{array}{c}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. Subsequent vectors $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_k\right\}_{{\cal B}}}$ are obtained by scaling horizontally by a factor of $1.3$ and scaling vertically by a factor $0.5$. Notice how the points move along a curve away from the origin becoming ever closer to the horizontal axis. After a very long time, $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_k\right\}_{{\cal B}}} \approx \left[\begin{array}{c}1.3^k\cdot2 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$.

To recover the behavior of the sequence $\kvecxzero, \kvecx_1, \kvecx_2, \ldots$, we change coordinate systems using the basis defined by $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$. Here, the points move along a curve away from the origin becoming ever closer to the line defined by $\kvecvone$.

Eventually, the vectors become practically indistinguishable from a scalar multiple of $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ since $\kvecxk\approx 1.3^k\cdot2\kvecvone$. This means that

$$ \kvecxk = \left[\begin{array}{r}R_k \\ S_k \end{array}\right] \approx 1.3^k\cdot 2\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1.3^k\cdot4 \\ 1.3^k\cdot2 \end{array}\right]. $$

This shows that

$$ R_k/S_k \approx (1.3^k\cdot 4) / (1.3^k\cdot 2) = 2 $$

so that we expect the population of species $R$ to eventually be about twice that of species $S$.

In addition, $\kvecx_{k+1} \approx 1.3\kvecxk$ so that $R_{k+1}\approx 1.3 R_k$ and $S_{k+1}\approx 1.3 S_k$, which tells us that both populations are multiplied by 1.3 every year meaning the annual growth rate for both populations is about 30%.

In the same way, we can consider other possible initial populations $\kvecxzero$ as shown in Figure 4.4.1. Regardless of $\kvecxzero$, the population vectors, in the coordinates defined by ${\cal B}$, are scaled horizontally by a factor of $1.3$ and vertically by a factor of $0.5$. The sequence of points $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_k\right\}_{{\cal B}}}$, called trajectories, move along the curves, as shown on the left. In the standard coordinate system, we see that the trajectories converge to the eigenspace $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{1.3}}$.

We conclude that, regardless of the initial populations, the ratio of the populations $R_k/S_k$ will approach 2 to 1 and that the growth rate for both populations approaches 30%. This example demonstrates the power of using eigenvalues and eigenvectors to rewrite the problem in terms of a new coordinate system. By doing so, we are able to predict the long-term behavior of the populations independently of the initial populations.

Diagrams like those shown in Figure 4.4.1 are called phase portraits. On the left of Figure 4.4.1 is the phase portrait of the diagonal matrix $\kD=\left[\begin{array}{rr}1.3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \\ \end{array}\right]$ while the right of that figure shows the phase portrait of $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr}0.9 & 0.8 \\ 0.2 & 0.9 \\ \end{array}\right]$. The phase portrait of $\kD$ is relatively easy to understand because it is determined only by the two eigenvalues. Once we have the phase portrait of $\kD$, however, the phase portrait of $\kA$ has a similar appearance with the eigenvectors $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\mathbf{v}_j}$ replacing the standard basis vectors $\kvecej$.

Classifying dynamical systems

In the previous example, we were able to make predictions about the behavior of trajectories $\kvecxk=\kAk\kvecxzero$ by considering the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix $\kA$. The next activity looks at a collection of matrices that demonstrate the types of behavior a $2\times2$ dynamical system can exhibit.

This activity demonstrates six possible types of dynamical systems, which are determined by the eigenvalues of $\kA$.

  • Suppose that $\kA$ has two real eigenvalues $\klambdaone$ and $\klambdatwo$ and that both $|\klambdaone|, |\klambdatwo| > 1$. In this case, any nonzero vector $\kvecxzero$ forms a trajectory that moves away from the origin so we say that the origin is a repellor. This is illustrated in Figure 4.4.2.
  • Suppose that $\kA$ has two real eigenvalues $\klambdaone$ and $\klambdatwo$ and that $|\klambdaone| > 1 > |\klambdatwo|$. In this case, most nonzero vectors $\kvecxzero$ form trajectories that converge to the eigenspace $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{\lambda_1}}$. In this case, we say that the origin is a saddle as illustrated in Figure 4.4.3.
  • Suppose that $\kA$ has two real eigenvalues $\klambdaone$ and $\klambdatwo$ and that both $|\klambdaone|, |\klambdatwo| < 1$. In this case, any nonzero vector $\kvecxzero$ forms a trajectory that moves into the origin so we say that the origin is an attractor. This is illustrated in Figure 4.4.4.
  • Suppose that $\kA$ has a complex eigenvalue $\klambda = a+bi$ where $|\klambda| > 1$. In this case, a nonzero vector $\kvecxzero$ forms a trajectory that spirals away from the origin. We say that the origin is a spiral repellor, as illustrated in Figure 4.4.5.
  • Suppose that $\kA$ has a complex eigenvalue $\klambda = a+bi$ where $|\klambda| = 1$. In this case, a nonzero vector $\kvecxzero$ forms a trajectory that moves on a closed curve around the origin. We say that the origin is a center, as illustrated in Figure 4.4.6.
  • Suppose that $\kA$ has a complex eigenvalue $\klambda = a+bi$ where $|\klambda| < 1$. In this case, a nonzero vector $\kvecxzero$ forms a trajectory that spirals into the origin. We say that the origin is a spiral attractor, as illustrated in Figure 4.4.7.

This list includes many types of expected behavior, but there are other possibilities if, for instance, one of the eigenvalues is 0. The next section explores the situation when one of the eigenvalues is 1.

A $3\times3$ system

Up to this point, we have focused on $2\times2$ systems. In fact, the general case is quite similar. As an example, consider a $3\times3$ system $\kvecx_{k+1}=\kA\kvecxk$ where the matrix $\kA$ has eigenvalues $\klambdaone = 0.6$, $\klambdatwo = 0.8$, and $\klambdathree=1.1$. Since the eigenvalues are real and distinct, there is a basis ${\cal B}$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$ so we can look at the trajectories $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\left\{\mathbf{x}_k\right\}_{{\cal B}}}$ in the coordinate system defined by ${\cal B}$. The phase portraits in Figure 4.4.8 show how some representative trajectories will evolve. We see that all the trajectories will converge into the eigenspace $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{1.1}}$.

In the same way, suppose we have a $3\times3$ system with complex eigenvalues $\klambda=0.8 \pm 0.5i$ and $\klambdathree=1.1$. Since the complex eigenvalues satisfy $|\klambda| < 1$, there is a two-dimensional subspace in which the trajectories spiral in toward the origin. The phase portraits in Figure 4.4.9 show some of the trajectories. Once again, we see that all the trajectories converge into the eigenspace $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{1.1}}$.
