Powers of a matrix

In Activity 4.1.3, we explored a car rental company with two locations and known probabilities that a car rented at one location was returned to the same location. If the distribution of cars on a given day is recorded by the vector $\kvecxk$, we found a matrix equation $\kvecx_{k+1}=\kA\kvecxk$ that determined how the distribution of cars changed from day to day. To understand what happens over a number of days, we could rewrite this expression in terms of the initial distribution $\kvecxzero$ as

$$ \kvecxk=\kAk\kvecxzero. $$

Making long-term predictions then requires us to understand how large powers of the matrix $\kA$ behave. This section will introduce some ideas for determining this behavior.

Introduction

The first example we considered in this chapter was the matrix $\kA=\left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right]$, which has eigenvectors $\kvecvone=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and associated eigenvalues $\klambdaone=3$ and $\klambdatwo=-1$. In Subsection 4.1.3, we described how $\kA$ is, in some sense, equivalent to the diagonal matrix $\kD = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\\ \end{array}\right]$.

This equivalence is summarized by Figure 4.3.1. The diagonal matrix $\kD$ has the geometric effect of stretching vectors horizontally by a factor of $3$ and flipping vectors vertically. The matrix $\kA$ has the geometric effect of stretching vectors by a factor of $3$ in the $\kvecvone$ direction and flipping them in the $\kvecvtwo$ direction. That is, the geometric effect of $\kA$ is the same as that of $\kD$ when viewed in a basis of eigenvectors of $\kA$.

Our goal in this section is to express this geometric observation in algebraic terms. In doing so, we will make precise the sense in which $\kA$ and $\kD$ are equivalent.

Diagonalization of matrices

When working with an $n\times n$ matrix $\kA$, Subsection 4.1.3 demonstrated the value of having a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$. In fact, Proposition 4.2.9 tells us that if the eigenvalues of $\kA$ are real and distinct, then there is a such a basis. As we’ll see later, there are other conditions on $\kA$ that guarantee a basis of eigenvectors. For now, suffice it to say that we can find a basis of eigenvectors for many matrices. With this assumption, we will see how the matrix $\kA$ is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\kD$.

More generally, suppose that we have an $n\times n$ matrix $\kA$ and that there is a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors $\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo,\ldots,{\mathbf v}_n$ of $\kA$ with associated eigenvalues $\klambdaone,\klambdatwo,\ldots,\lambda_n$. If we use the eigenvectors to form the matrix

$$ \kP = \begin{bmatrix} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo & \cdots & {\mathbf v}_n \end{bmatrix} $$

and the eigenvalues to form the diagonal matrix

$$ \kD = \left[\begin{array}{cccc} \klambdaone & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & \klambdatwo & \ldots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \ldots & \lambda_n \\ \end{array}\right] $$

and apply the same reasoning demonstrated in the activity, we find that $\kA\kP = \kP\kD$ and hence

$$ \kA=\kP\kD\kPinv. $$

We have now seen the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3.2

If $\kA$ is an $n\times n$ matrix and there is a basis $\{\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo,\ldots,{\mathbf v}_n\}$ of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$ having associated eigenvalues $\klambdaone, \klambdatwo, \ldots, \lambda_n$, then we can write $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$ where $\kD$ is the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of $\kA$

$$ \kD = \left[\begin{array}{cccc} \klambdaone & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & \klambdatwo & \ldots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \ldots & \lambda_n \\ \end{array}\right] $$

and the matrix $\kP = \left[\begin{array}{cccc} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo & \ldots & {\mathbf v}_n \end{array}\right]$.

Example 4.3.3

We have seen that $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvectors $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kvecvtwo=\left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ with associated eigenvalues $\klambdaone = 3$ and $\klambdatwo = -1$. Forming the matrices

$$ \kP = \begin{bmatrix} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix},~~~ \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ \end{bmatrix}, $$

we see that $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$.

This is the sense in which we mean that $\kA$ is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\kD$. The expression $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$ says that $\kA$, expressed in the basis defined by the columns of $\kP$, has the same geometric effect as $\kD$, expressed in the standard basis $\kveceone, \kvecetwo,\ldots,{\mathbf e}_n$.

Definition 4.3.4

We say that the matrix $\kA$ is diagonalizable if there is a diagonal matrix $\kD$ and invertible matrix $\kP$ such that

$$ \kA = \kP\kD\kPinv. $$

Example 4.3.5

We will try to find a diagonalization of $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} -5 & 6 \\ -3 & 4 \\ \end{array}\right]$ whose characteristic equation is

$$ \det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = (-5-\klambda)(4-\klambda)+18 = (-2-\klambda)(1-\klambda) = 0. $$

This shows that the eigenvalues of $\kA$ are $\klambdaone = -2$ and $\klambdatwo = 1$.

By constructing $\text{Nul}(\kA-(-2)\kI)$, we find a basis for $E_{-2}$ consisting of the vector $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. Similarly, a basis for $\kEone$ consists of the vector $\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. This shows that we can construct a basis $\{\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo\}$ of ${\mathbb R}^2$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$.

We now form the matrices

$$ \kD = \left[\begin{array}{rr} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right],\qquad \kP = \left[\begin{array}{cc} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] $$

and verify that

$$ \kP\kD\kPinv = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \\ \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} -5 & 6 \\ -3 & 4 \\ \end{array}\right] = \kA. $$

There are, in fact, many ways to diagonalize $\kA$. For instance, we could change the order of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors and write

$$ \kD = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right],\qquad \kP = \left[\begin{array}{cc} \kvecvtwo & \kvecvone \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

If we choose a different basis for the eigenspaces, we will also find a different matrix $\kP$ that diagonalizes $\kA$. The point is that there are many ways in which $\kA$ can be written in the form $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$.

Example 4.3.6

We will try to find a diagonalization of $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & 4 \\ -1 & 4 \\ \end{array}\right]$.

Once again, we find the eigenvalues by solving the characteristic equation:

$$ \det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = -\klambda(4-\klambda) + 4 = (2-\klambda)^2 = 0. $$

In this case, there is a single eigenvalue $\klambda=2$.

We find a basis for the eigenspace $E_2$ by describing $\text{Nul}(\kA-2\kI)$:

$$ \kA-2\kI = \left[\begin{array}{rr} -2 & 4 \\ -1 & 2 \\ \end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

This shows that the eigenspace $E_2$ is one-dimensional with $\kvecvone=\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ forming a basis.

In this case, there is not a basis of ${\mathbb R}^2$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$, which tells us that $\kA$ is not diagonalizable.

In fact, if we only know that $\kA = \kP\kD\kPinv$, we can say that the columns of $\kP$ are eigenvectors of $\kA$ and that the diagonal entries of $\kD$ are the associated eigenvalues.

Proposition 4.3.7

An $n\times n$ matrix $\kA$ is diagonalizable if and only if there is a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$.

Example 4.3.8

Suppose we know that $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$ where

$$ \kD = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right],\qquad \kP = \left[\begin{array}{cc} \kvecvtwo & \kvecvone \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

The columns of $\kP$ form eigenvectors of $\kA$ so that $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with eigenvalue $\klambdaone = 2$ and $\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\klambdatwo=-2$.

We can verify this by computing

$$ \kA = \kP\kD\kPinv = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 6 & -4 \\ 8 & -6 \\ \end{array}\right] $$

and checking that $\kA\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]=2\kvecvone$ and $\kA\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] = -2\kvecvtwo$.

Powers of a diagonalizable matrix

In several earlier examples, we have been interested in computing powers of a given matrix. For instance, in Activity 4.1.3, we had the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 \\ \end{array}\right]$ and an initial vector $\kvecxzero=\left[\begin{array}{c}1000 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$, and we wanted to compute

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecx_1 & {}={} \kA\kvecxzero \\ \kvecx_2 & {}={} \kA\kvecx_1 = \kA^2\kvecxzero \\ \kvecx_3 & {}={} \kA\kvecx_2 = \kA^3\kvecxzero\text{.} \\ \end{aligned} $$

In particular, we wanted to find $\kvecxk=\kAk\kvecxzero$ and determine what happens as $k$ becomes very large. If a matrix $\kA$ is diagonalizable, writing $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$ can help us understand powers of $\kA$ more easily.

If $\kA$ is diagonalizable, the activity demonstrates that any power of $\kA$ is as well.

Proposition 4.3.9

If $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$, then $\kAk = \kP\kDk\kPinv$. When $\kA$ is invertible, we also have $\kAinv = \kP\kD^{-1}\kPinv$.

Example 4.3.10

Let’s revisit Activity 4.1.3 where we had the matrix $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.2 & 0.4 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ and the initial vector $\kvecxzero = \left[\begin{array}{c}1000 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$. We were interested in understanding the sequence of vectors $\kvecx_{k+1} = \kA\kvecxk$, which means that $\kvecxk = \kAk\kvecxzero$.

We can verify that $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}3 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ are eigenvectors of $\kA$ having associated eigenvalues $\klambdaone=1$ and $\klambdatwo = 0.2$. This means that $\kA = \kP\kD\kPinv$ where

$$ \kP = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix},~~~ \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

Therefore, the powers of $\kA$ have the form $\kAk = \kP\kDk\kPinv$.

Notice that $\kDk = \begin{bmatrix} 1^k & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2^k \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2^k \end{bmatrix}$. As $k$ increases, $0.2^k$ becomes closer and closer to zero. This means that for very large powers $k$, we have

$$ \kDk \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} $$

and therefore

$$ \kAk = \kP\kDk\kPinv \approx \kP\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}\kPinv = \begin{bmatrix} \frac 34 & \frac 34 \\ \frac 14 & \frac 14 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Beginning with the vector $\kvecxzero = \left[\begin{array}{c}1000 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$, we find that $\kvecxk = \kAk\kvecxzero\approx \left[\begin{array}{r}750 \\ 250 \end{array}\right]$ when $k$ is very large.

Similarity and complex eigenvalues

We have been interested in diagonalizing a matrix $\kA$ because doing so relates a matrix $\kA$ to a simpler diagonal matrix $\kD$. In particular, the effect of multiplying a vector by $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv$, viewed in the basis defined by the columns of $\kP$, is the same as the effect of multiplying by $\kD$ in the standard basis.

While many matrices are diagonalizable, there are some that are not. For example, if a matrix has complex eigenvalues, it is not possible to find a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors, which means that the matrix is not diagonalizable. In this case, however, we can still relate the matrix to a simpler form that explains the geometric effect this matrix has on vectors.

Definition 4.3.11

We say that $\kA$ is similar to $\kB$ if there is an invertible matrix $\kP$ such that $\kA = \kP\kB\kPinv$.

Notice that a matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it is similar to a diagonal matrix. In case a matrix $\kA$ has complex eigenvalues, we will find a simpler matrix $\kC$ that is similar to $\kA$ and note that $\kA=\kP\kC\kPinv$ has the same effect, when viewed in the basis defined by the columns of $\kP$, as $\kC$, when viewed in the standard basis.

To begin, suppose that $\kA$ is a $2\times2$ matrix having a complex eigenvalue $\klambda = a+bi$. It turns out that $\kA$ is similar to $\kC=\begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \\ \end{bmatrix}$.

The next activity shows that $\kC$ has a simple geometric effect on ${\mathbb R}^2$. First, however, we will use polar coordinates to rewrite $\kC$. As shown in the figure, the point $(a,b)$ defines $r$, the distance from the origin, and $\theta$, the angle formed with the positive horizontal axis. We then have

$$ \begin{aligned} a & {}={} r\cos\theta \\ b & {}={} r\sin\theta\text{.} \\ \end{aligned} $$

Notice that the Pythagorean theorem says that $r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

If the $2\times2$ matrix $\kA$ has a complex eigenvalue $\klambda = a + bi$, it turns out that $\kA$ is always similar to the matrix $\kC = \left[\begin{array}{rr} a & -b \\ b & a \\ \end{array}\right],$ whose geometric effect on vectors can be described in terms of a rotation and a scaling. There is, in fact, a method for finding the matrix $\kP$ so that $\kA=\kP\kC\kPinv$ that we’ll see in Exercise 4.3.8. For now, we note that $\kA$ has the same geometric effect as $\kC$, when viewed in the basis provided by the columns of $\kP$. We will put this fact to use in the next section to understand certain dynamical systems.

Proposition 4.3.12

If $\kA$ is a $2\times2$ matrix with a complex eigenvalue $\klambda = a + bi$, then $\kA$ is similar to $\kC = \begin{bmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \\ \end{bmatrix}$; that is, there is a matrix $\kP$ such that $\kA= \kP\kC\kPinv$.
