An algebraic method

The last section introduced eigenvalues and eigenvectors, presented the underlying geometric intuition behind their definition, and demonstrated their use in understanding the long-term behavior of certain systems. We will now develop a more algebraic understanding of eigenvalues and eigenvectors. In particular, we will find an algebraic method for determining the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a square matrix.

Introduction

The characteristic polynomial

We will first see that the eigenvalues of a square matrix appear as the roots of a particular polynomial. To begin, notice that we originally defined an eigenvector as a nonzero vector $\kvecv$ that satisfies the equation $\kA\kvecv = \klambda\kvecv$. We will rewrite this as

In other words, an eigenvector $\kvecv$ is a solution of the homogeneous equation $(\kA-\klambda \kI)\kvecv=\kveczero$. This puts us in the familiar territory explored in the next activity.

This activity demonstrates a technique that enables us to find the eigenvalues of a square matrix $\kA$. Since an eigenvalue $\klambda$ is a scalar for which the equation $(\kA-\klambda \kI)\kvecv = \kveczero$ has a nonzero solution, it must be the case that $\kA-\klambda \kI$ is not invertible. Therefore, its determinant is zero. This gives us the equation

$$ \det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = 0 $$

whose solutions are the eigenvalues of $\kA$. This equation is called the characteristic equation of $\kA$.

Example 4.2.1

If we write the characteristic equation for the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} -4 & 4 \\ -12 & 10 \\ \end{array}\right]$, we see that

$$ \begin{aligned} \det(\kA-\klambda \kI) & {}={} 0 \\ \\ \det \left[\begin{array}{rr} -4 - \klambda & 4 \\ -12 & 10 - \klambda \\ \end{array}\right] & {}={} 0 \\ \\ (-4-\klambda)(10-\klambda) + 48 & {}={} 0 \\ \klambda^2-6\klambda + 8 {}={} 0 \\ (\klambda-4)(\klambda-2) {}={} 0\text{.} \\ \end{aligned} $$

This shows us that the eigenvalues are $\klambda = 4$ and $\klambda=2$.

In general, the expression $\det(\kA-\klambda \kI)$ is a polynomial in $\klambda$, which is called the characteristic polynomial of $\kA$. If $\kA$ is an $n\times n$ matrix, the degree of the characteristic polynomial is $n$. For instance, if $\kA$ is a $2\times2$ matrix, then $\det(\kA-\klambda \kI)$ is a quadratic polynomial; if $\kA$ is a $3\times3$ matrix, then $\det(\kA-\klambda \kI)$ is a cubic polynomial.

The matrix in Example 4.2.1 has a characteristic polynomial with two real and distinct roots. This will not always be the case, as demonstrated in the next two examples.

Example 4.2.2

Consider the matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -1\\ 4 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$, whose characteristic equation is

$$ \klambda^2 - 6\klambda + 9 = (\klambda-3)^2 = 0. $$

In this case, the characteristic polynomial has one real root, which means that this matrix has a single real eigenvalue, $\klambda = 3$.

Example 4.2.3

To find the eigenvalues of a triangular matrix, we remember that the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of the entries on the diagonal. For instance, the following triangular matrix has the characteristic equation

$$ \begin{aligned} \det\left( \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 4 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ \end{array}\right] -\klambda \kI\right) & {}={} \det \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 4-\klambda & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -2-\klambda & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3-\klambda \\ \end{array}\right] \\ \\ & {}={}(4-\klambda)(-2-\klambda)(3-\klambda) = 0\text{,} \end{aligned} $$

showing that the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries $\klambda = 4,-2,3$.

Finding eigenvectors

Now that we can find the eigenvalues of a square matrix $\kA$ by solving the characteristic equation $\det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = 0$, we will turn to the question of finding the eigenvectors associated to an eigenvalue $\klambda$. The key, as before, is to note that an eigenvector is a nonzero solution to the homogeneous equation $(\kA-\klambda \kI)\kvecv = \kveczero$. In other words, the eigenvectors associated to an eigenvalue $\klambda$ form the null space $\kNul(\kA-\klambda \kI)$.

This shows that the eigenvectors associated to an eigenvalue form a subspace of ${\mathbb R}^n$. We will denote the subspace of eigenvectors of a matrix $\kA$ associated to the eigenvalue $\klambda$ by $\kElambda$ and note that

$$ \kElambda = \kNul(\kA-\klambda \kI). $$

We say that $\kElambda$ is the eigenspace of $\kA$ associated to the eigenvalue $\klambda$.

Once we find an eigenvalue of a matrix $\kA$, describing the associated eigenspace $\kElambda$ amounts to the familiar task of describing the null space $\kNul(\kA-\klambda \kI)$.

Example 4.2.4

Revisiting the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} -4 & 4 \\ -12 & 10 \\ \end{array}\right]$ from Example 4.2.1, we recall that we found eigenvalues $\klambda = 4$ and $\klambda = 2$.

Considering the eigenvalue $\klambda = 4$, we have

$$ \kA -4\kI = \begin{bmatrix} -8 & 4 \\ -12 & 6 \\ \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1/2 \\ 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

Since the eigenvectors $\kvecv = \left[\begin{array}{r}v_1 \\ v_2 \end{array}\right]$ are the solutions of the equation $(\kA-4\kI)\kvecv=\kveczero$, we see that they are determined by the single equation $v_1-\frac12 v_2 = 0$ or $v_1 = \frac12v_2$. Therefore the eigenvectors in $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_4}$ have the form

$$ \kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}v_1 \\ v_2 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{c}\frac12 v_2 \\ v_2 \end{array}\right] = v_2\left[\begin{array}{c}1/2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]. $$

In other words, $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_4}$ is a one-dimensional subspace of ${\mathbb R}^2$ with basis vector $\left[\begin{array}{c}1/2 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ or basis vector $\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]$. In the same way, we find that a basis for the eigenspace $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_2}$ is $\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 3 \end{array}\right]$.

We note that, for this matrix, it is possible to construct a basis of ${\mathbb R}^2$ consisting of eigenvectors, namely,

$$ {\cal B} = \left\{\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 3 \end{array}\right]\right\}. $$

Example 4.2.5

Consider the matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ whose characteristic equation is

$$ \det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = \klambda^2 - 4\klambda + 4 = (\klambda-2)^2 =0. $$

There is a single eigenvalue $\klambda= 2$, and we find that

$$ \kA - 2\klambda = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

Therefore, the eigenspace $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_2} = \kNul(\kA-2\kI)$ is one-dimensional with a basis vector $\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$.

Example 4.2.6

If $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$, then

$$ \det(\kA - \klambda \kI) = (\klambda+1)^2 = 0, $$

which implies that there is a single eigenvalue $\klambda=-1$. We find that

$$ \kA-(-1)\kI = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}, $$

which says that every two-dimensional vector $\kvecv$ satisfies $(\kA-(-1)\kI)\kvecv = \kveczero$. Therefore, every vector is an eigenvector and so $\htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{-1}} = {\mathbb R}^2$. This eigenspace is two-dimensional.

We can see this in another way. The matrix transformation defined by $\kA$ rotates vectors by $180^\circ$, which says that $\kA\kvecx = -\kvecx$ for every vector $\kvecx$. In other words, every two-dimensional vector is an eigenvector with associated eigenvalue $\klambda=-1$.

These last two examples illustrate two types of behavior when there is a single eigenvalue. In one case, we are able to construct a basis of ${\mathbb R}^2$ using eigenvectors; in the other, we are not. We will explore this behavior more in the next subsection.

Summary · A check on our work

When finding eigenvalues and their associated eigenvectors in this way, we first find eigenvalues $\klambda$ by solving the characteristic equation. If $\klambda$ is a solution to the characteristic equation, then $\kA-\klambda \kI$ is not invertible and, consequently, $\kA-\klambda \kI$ must contain a row without a pivot position.

This serves as a check on our work. If we row reduce $\kA-\klambda \kI$ and find the identity matrix, then we have made an error either in solving the characteristic equation or in finding $\kNul(\kA-\klambda \kI)$.

The characteristic polynomial and the dimension of eigenspaces

Given a square $n\times n$ matrix $\kA$, we saw in the previous section the value of being able to express any vector in ${\mathbb R}^n$ as a linear combination of eigenvectors of $\kA$. For this reason, Question 4.1.8 asks when we can construct a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors. We will explore this question more fully now.

As we saw above, the eigenvalues of $\kA$ are the solutions of the characteristic equation $\det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = 0$. The examples we have considered demonstrate some different types of behavior. For instance, we have seen the characteristic equations

  • $(4-\klambda)(-2-\klambda)(3-\klambda) = 0$, which has real and distinct roots,
  • $(2-\klambda)^2 = 0$, which has repeated roots, and
  • $\klambda^2 +1 = (i-\klambda)(-i-\klambda) = 0$, which has complex roots.

If $\kA$ is an $n\times n$ matrix, then the characteristic polynomial is a degree $n$ polynomial, and this means that it has $n$ roots. Therefore, the characteristic equation can be written as

$$ \det(\kA-\klambda \kI) = (\klambdaone - \klambda)(\klambdatwo-\klambda)\ldots(\htmlClass{kv-eigenvalue}{\lambda_n}-\klambda) = 0 $$

giving eigenvalues $\klambdaone, \klambdatwo,\ldots,\htmlClass{kv-eigenvalue}{\lambda_n}$. As we have seen, some of the eigenvalues may be complex. Moreover, some of the eigenvalues may appear in this list more than once. However, we can always write the characteristic equation in the form

$$ (\klambdaone-\klambda)^{m_1} (\klambdatwo-\klambda)^{m_2} \ldots (\htmlClass{kv-eigenvalue}{\lambda_p}-\klambda)^{m_p} = 0. $$

The number of times that $\klambdaj - \klambda$ appears as a factor in the characteristic polynomial, is called the multiplicity of the eigenvalue $\klambdaj$.

Example 4.2.7

We have seen that the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \\ \end{array}\right]$ has the characteristic equation $(2-\klambda)^2 = 0$. This matrix has a single eigenvalue $\klambda = 2$, which has multiplicity $2$.

Example 4.2.8

If a matrix has the characteristic equation

$$ (4-\klambda)^2(-5-\klambda)(1-\klambda)^7(3-\klambda)^2 = 0, $$

then that matrix has four eigenvalues: $\klambda=4$ having multiplicity 2; $\klambda=-5$ having multiplicity 1; $\klambda=1$ having multiplicity 7; and $\klambda=3$ having multiplicity 2. The degree of the characteristic polynomial is the sum of the multiplicities $2+1+7+2 = 12$ so this matrix must be a $12\times12$ matrix.

The multiplicities of the eigenvalues are important because they influence the dimension of the eigenspaces. We know that the dimension of an eigenspace must be at least one; the following proposition also tells us the dimension of an eigenspace can be no larger than the multiplicity of its associated eigenvalue.

Proposition 4.2.9

If $\klambda$ is a real eigenvalue of the matrix $\kA$ with multiplicity $m$, then

$$ 1 \leq \dim~\kElambda \leq m. $$

Example 4.2.10

The diagonal matrix $\left[\begin{array}{rr} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ \end{array}\right]$ has the characteristic equation $(-1-\klambda)^2 = 0$. There is a single eigenvalue $\klambda = -1$ having multiplicity $m = 2$, and we saw earlier that $\dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{-1}} = 2 \leq m = 2$.

Example 4.2.11

The matrix $\left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \\ \end{array}\right]$ has the characteristic equation $(2-\klambda)^2 = 0$. This tells us that there is a single eigenvalue $\klambda = 2$ having multiplicity $m = 2$. In contrast with the previous example, we have $\dim~ \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_2} = 1 \leq m = 2$.

Example 4.2.12

We saw earlier that the matrix $\left[\begin{array}{rrr} 4 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \\ \end{array}\right]$ has the characteristic equation

$$ (4-\klambda)(-2-\klambda)(3-\klambda)=0. $$

There are three eigenvalues $\klambda=3,-2,1$ each having multiplicity $1$. By the proposition, we are guaranteed that the dimension of each eigenspace is $1$; that is,

$$ \dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_3} = \dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{-2}} = \dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_1} = 1. $$

It turns out that this is enough to guarantee that there is a basis of ${\mathbb R}^3$ consisting of eigenvectors.

Example 4.2.13

If a $12\times12$ matrix has the characteristic equation

$$ (4-\klambda)^2(-5-\klambda)(1-\klambda)^7(3-\klambda)^2 = 0, $$

we know there are four eigenvalues $\klambda=4,-5,1,3$. Without more information, all we can say about the dimensions of the eigenspaces is

$$ \begin{aligned} 1 \leq \dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_4} & {}\leq{} 2 \\ 1 \leq \dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{-5}} & {}\leq{} 1 \\ 1 \leq \dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_1} & {}\leq{} 7 \\ 1 \leq \dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_3} & {}\leq{} 2\text{.} \\ \end{aligned} $$

We can guarantee that $\dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{-5}} = 1$, but we cannot be more specific about the dimensions of the other eigenspaces.

Fortunately, if we have an $n\times n$ matrix, it frequently happens that the characteristic equation has the form

$$ (\klambdaone-\klambda) (\klambdatwo-\klambda) \ldots (\htmlClass{kv-eigenvalue}{\lambda_n}-\klambda) = 0 $$

where there are $n$ distinct real eigenvalues, each of which has multiplicity $1$. In this case, the dimension of each of the eigenspaces $\dim \htmlClass{kv-region}{E_{\lambda_j}} = 1$. With a little work, it can be seen that choosing a basis vector $\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\mathbf{v}_j}$ for each of the eigenspaces produces a basis for ${\mathbb R}^n$. We therefore have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2.14

If $\kA$ is an $n\times n$ matrix having $n$ distinct real eigenvalues, then there is a basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\kA$.

This proposition provides one answer to our Question 4.1.8. The next activity explores this question further.
