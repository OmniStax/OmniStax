From dynamical systems to Markov chains

In the last section, we used our understanding of eigenvalues and eigenvectors to describe the long-term behavior of some discrete dynamical systems. The state of the system, which could record, say, the populations of a few interacting species, at one time is described by a vector $\kvecxk$. The state vector then evolves according to a linear rule $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \kA\kvecxk$.

This section continues this exploration by looking at Markov chains, which form a specific type of discrete dynamical system. For instance, we could be interested in a rental car company that rents cars from several locations. From one day to the next, the number of cars at different locations can change, but the total number of cars stays the same. Once again, an understanding of eigenvalues and eigenvectors will help us make predictions about the long-term behavior of the system.

A first example

In the preview activity, the distribution of rental cars was described by the discrete dynamical system

$$ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \kA\kvecxk=\left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ 0.2 & 0.6 \\ \end{array}\right] \kvecxk. $$

This matrix has some special properties. First, each entry represents the probability that a car rented at one location is returned to another. For instance, there is an 80% chance that a car rented at $P$ is returned to $P$, which explains the entry of 0.8 in the upper left corner. Therefore, the entries of the matrix are between 0 and 1.

Second, a car rented at one location must be returned to one of the locations. For example, since 80% of the cars rented at $P$ are returned to $P$, it follows that the other 20% of cars rented at $P$ are returned to $Q$. This implies that the entries in each column must add to 1. This will occur frequently in our discussion so we introduce the following definitions.

Definition 4.5.1

A vector whose entries are nonnegative and add to 1 is called a probability vector. A square matrix whose columns are probability vectors is called a stochastic matrix.

The previous activity illustrates some important points that we wish to emphasize.

First, to determine $P_{k+1}$, we note that in election $k+1$, party $P$ retains 60% of its voters from the previous election and adds 20% of those who voted for party $R$. In this way, we see that

$$ \begin{alignedat}{6} P_{k+1} & {}={} 0.6P_k & & & + 0.2 R_k \\ Q_{k+1} & {}={} 0.4P_k & {}+{} & 0.6Q_k & {}+{} 0.2R_k \\ R_{k+1} & {}={} & {}{} & 0.4Q_k & {}+{} 0.6R_k\\ \end{alignedat} $$

We therefore define the matrix

$$ \kA = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 0.6 & 0 & 0.2 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 & 0.2 \\ 0 & 0.4 & 0.6 \\ \end{array}\right] $$

and note that $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \kA\kvecxk$.

If we consider the first column of $\kA$, we see that the entries represent the percentages of party $P$’s voters in the last election who vote for each of the three parties in the next election. Since everyone who voted for party $P$ previously votes for one of the three parties in the next election, the sum of these percentages must be 1. This is true for each of the columns of $\kA$, which explains why $\kA$ is a stochastic matrix.

We begin with the vector $\kvecxzero = \left[\begin{array}{r}0.4 \\ 0.3 \\ 0.3 \end{array}\right]$, the entries of which represent the percentage of voters voting for each of the three parties. Since every voter votes for one of the three parties, the sum of these entries must be 1, which means that $\kvecxzero$ is a probability vector. We then find that

$$ \begin{array}{cccc} \mathbf{x}_1=\left[\begin{array}{r}0.300 \\ 0.400 \\ 0.300 \end{array}\right],& \mathbf{x}_2=\left[\begin{array}{r}0.240 \\ 0.420 \\ 0.340 \end{array}\right],& \mathbf{x}_3=\left[\begin{array}{r}0.212 \\ 0.416 \\ 0.372 \end{array}\right],& \ldots,\\ \\ \mathbf{x}_5=\left[\begin{array}{r}0.199 \\ 0.404 \\ 0.397 \end{array}\right],& \ldots,& \mathbf{x}_{10}=\left[\begin{array}{r}0.200 \\ 0.400 \\ 0.400 \end{array}\right],& \ldots \\ \end{array} $$

Notice that the vectors $\kvecxk$ are also probability vectors and that the sequence $\kvecxk$ seems to be converging to $\left[\begin{array}{r}0.2 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.4 \end{array}\right]$. It is this behavior that we would like to understand more fully by investigating the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\kA$.

We find that the eigenvalues of $\kA$ are

$$ \klambdaone = 1, \qquad \klambdatwo = 0.4 + 0.2i, \qquad\klambdathree = 0.4-0.2i. $$

Notice that if $\kvecv$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with associated eigenvalue $\klambdaone=1$, then $\kA\kvecv = 1\kvecv = \kvecv$. That is, $\kvecv$ is unchanged when we multiply it by $\kA$.

Otherwise, we have $\kA=\kP\kE\kPinv$ where

$$ \kE = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.4 & -0.2 \\ 0 & 0.2 & 0.4 \\ \end{array}\right] $$

Notice that $|\klambdatwo| = |\klambdathree| < 1$ so the trajectories $\kvecxk$ spiral into the eigenspace $\kEone$ as indicated in the figure.

This tells us that the sequence $\kvecxk$ converges to a vector in $\kEone$. In the usual way, we see that $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{array}\right]$ is a basis vector for $\kEone$ because $\kA\kvecv = \kvecv$ so we expect that $\kvecxk$ will converge to a scalar multiple of $\kvecv$. Indeed, since the vectors $\kvecxk$ are probability vectors, we expect them to converge to a probability vector in $\kEone$.

We can find the probability vector in $\kEone$ by finding the appropriate scalar multiple of $\kvecv$. Notice that $c\kvecv = \left[\begin{array}{r}c \\ 2c \\ 2c \end{array}\right]$ is a probability vector when $c+2c+2c=5c = 1$, which implies that $c = 1/5$. Therefore, $\kvecq=\left[\begin{array}{r}0.2 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.4 \end{array}\right]$ is the unique probability vector in $\kEone$. Since the sequence $\kvecxk$ converges to a probability vector in $\kEone$, we see that $\kvecxk$ converges to $\kvecq$, which agrees with the computations we showed above.

The role of the eigenvalues is important in this example. Since $\klambdaone=1$, we can find a probability vector $\kvecq$ that is unchanged by multiplication by $\kA$. Also, the other eigenvalues satisfy $|\klambdaj| < 1$, which means that all the trajectories get pulled in to the eigenspace $\kEone$. Since $\kvecxk$ is a sequence of probability vectors, these vectors converge to the probability vector $\kvecq$ as they are pulled into $\kEone$.

Markov chains

If we have a stochastic matrix $\kA$ and a probability vector $\kvecxzero$, we can form the sequence $\kvecxk$ where $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \kA \kvecxk$. We call this sequence of vectors a Markov chain. Exercise 4.5.6 explains why we can guarantee that the vectors $\kvecxk$ are probability vectors.

In the example that studied voting patterns, we constructed a Markov chain that described how the percentages of voters choosing different parties changed from one election to the next. We saw that the Markov chain converges to $\kvecq=\left[\begin{array}{r}0.2 \\ 0.4 \\ 0.4 \end{array}\right]$, a probability vector in the eigenspace $\kEone$. In other words, $\kvecq$ is a probability vector that is unchanged under multiplication by $\kA$; that is, $\kA\kvecq = \kvecq$. This implies that, after a long time, 20% of voters choose party $P$, 40% choose $Q$, and 40% choose $R$.

Definition 4.5.2

If $\kA$ is a stochastic matrix, we say that a probability vector $\kvecq$ is a steady-state or stationary vector if $\kA\kvecq = \kvecq$.

An important question that arises from our previous example is

Question 4.5.3

If $\kA$ is a stochastic matrix and $\kvecxk$ a Markov chain, does $\kvecxk$ converge to a steady-state vector?

As this activity implies, the eigenvalues of a stochastic matrix tell us whether a Markov chain will converge to a steady-state vector. Here are a few important facts about the eigenvalues of a stochastic matrix.

  • As is demonstrated in Exercise 4.5.8, $\klambda=1$ is an eigenvalue of any stochastic matrix. We usually order the eigenvalues so it is the first eigenvalue meaning that $\klambdaone=1$.
  • All other eigenvalues satisfy the property that $|\klambdaj| \leq 1$.
  • Any stochastic matrix has at least one steady-state vector $\kvecq$.

As illustrated in the activity, a Markov chain could fail to converge to a steady-state vector if $|\klambdatwo| = 1$. This happens for the matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right]$, whose eigenvalues are $\klambdaone=1$ and $\klambdatwo = -1$.

However, if all but the first eigenvalue satisfy $|\klambdaj|< 1$, then there is a unique steady-state vector $\kvecq$ and any Markov chain will converge to $\kvecq$. This was the case for the matrix $\kB = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.6 & 0.7 \\ \end{array}\right]$, whose eigenvalues are $\klambdaone=1$ and $\klambdatwo = 0.1$. In this case, any Markov chain will converge to the unique steady-state vector $\kvecq = \left[\begin{array}{r}\frac13 \\ \frac23 \end{array}\right]$.

In this way, we see that the eigenvalues of a stochastic matrix tell us whether a Markov chain will converge to a steady-state vector. However, it is somewhat inconvenient to compute the eigenvalues to answer this question. Is there some way to conclude that every Markov chain will converge to a steady-state vector without actually computing the eigenvalues? It turns out that there is a simple condition on the matrix $\kA$ that guarantees this.

Definition 4.5.4

We say that a matrix $\kA$ is positive if either $\kA$ or some power $\kA^k$ has all positive entries.

Example 4.5.5

The matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right]$ is not positive. We can see this because some of the entries of $\kA$ are zero and therefore not positive. In addition, we see that $\kA^2 = \kI$, $\kA^3 = \kA$ and so forth. Therefore, every power of $\kA$ also has some zero entries, which means that $\kA$ is not positive.

The matrix $\kB = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.6 & 0.7 \\ \end{array}\right]$ is positive because every entry of $\kB$ is positive.

Also, the matrix $\kC = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & 0.5 \\ 1 & 0.5 \\ \end{array}\right]$ clearly has a zero entry. However, $\kC^2 = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0.5 & 0.25 \\ 0.5 & 0.75 \\ \end{array}\right]$, which has all positive entries. Therefore, we see that $\kC$ is a positive matrix.

Positive matrices are important because of the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5.6 · Perron-Frobenius

If $\kA$ is a positive stochastic matrix, then the eigenvalues satisfy $\klambdaone=1$ and $|\klambdaj| < 1$ for $j> 1$. This means that $\kA$ has a unique positive, steady-state vector $\kvecq$ and that every Markov chain defined by $\kA$ will converge to $\kvecq$.

Google’s PageRank algorithm

Markov chains and the Perron-Frobenius theorem are the central ingredients in Google’s PageRank algorithm, developed by Google to assess the quality of web pages.

Suppose we enter “linear algebra” into Google’s search engine. Google responds by telling us there are 138 million web pages containing those terms. On the first page, however, there are links to ten web pages that Google judges to have the highest quality and to be the ones we are most likely to be interested in. How does Google assess the quality of web pages?

At the time this is being written, Google is tracking 35 trillion web pages. Clearly, this is too many for humans to evaluate. Plus, human evaluators may inject their own biases into their evaluations, perhaps even unintentionally. Google’s idea is to use the structure of the Internet to assess the quality of web pages without any human intervention. For instance, if a web page has quality content, other web pages will link to it. This means that the number of links to a page reflect the quality of that page. In addition, we would expect a page to have even higher quality content if those links are coming from pages that are themselves assessed to have high quality. Simply said, if many quality pages link to a page, that page must itself be of high quality. This is the essence of the PageRank algorithm, which we introduce in the next activity.

This activity shows us two ways to find the PageRank vector. In the first, we determine a steady-state vector directly by finding a description of the eigenspace $\kEone$ and then finding the appropriate scalar multiple of a basis vector that gives us the steady-state vector. To find a description of the eigenspace $\kEone$, however, we need to find the null space $\kNul(\kG-\kI)$. Remember that the real Internet has 35 trillion pages so finding $\kNul(\kG-\kI)$ requires us to row reduce a matrix with 35 trillion rows and columns. As we saw in Subsection 1.3.3, that is not computationally feasible.

As suggested by the activity, the second way to find the PageRank vector is to use a Markov chain that converges to the PageRank vector. Since multiplying a vector by a matrix is significantly less work than row reducing the matrix, this approach is computationally feasible, and it is, in fact, how Google computes the PageRank vector.

The Perron-Frobenius theorem Theorem 4.5.6 tells us that a Markov chain $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\kG\kvecxk$ converges to a unique steady-state vector when the matrix $\kG$ is positive. This means that $\kG$ or some power of $\kG$ should have only positive entries. Clearly, this is not the case for the matrix formed from the Internet in Figure 4.5.9.

We can understand the problem with the Internet shown in Figure 4.5.10 by adding a box around some of the pages as shown in Figure 4.5.11. Here we see that the pages outside of the box give up all of their PageRank to the pages inside the box. This is not desirable because the PageRanks of the pages outside of the box are found to be zero. Once again, the Google matrix $\kG$ is not a positive matrix.

Google solves this problem by slightly modifying the Google matrix $\kG$ to obtain a positive matrix $\kGprime$. To understand this, think of the entries in the Google matrix as giving the probability that an Internet user follows a link from one page of another. To create a positive matrix, we will allow that user to randomly jump to any other page on the Internet with a small probability.

To make sense of this, suppose that there are $n$ pages on our internet. The matrix

$$ \kHn = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr} \frac1n & \frac1n & \ldots & \frac1n \\ \frac1n & \frac1n & \ldots & \frac1n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac1n & \frac1n & \ldots & \frac1n \\ \end{array}\right] $$

is a positive stochastic matrix describing a process where we can move from any page to another with equal probability. To form the modified Google matrix $\kGprime$, we choose a parameter $\alpha$ that is used to mix $\kG$ and $\kHn$ together; that is, $\kGprime$ is the positive stochastic matrix

$$ \kGprime = \alpha \kG +(1-\alpha)\kHn. $$

In practice, it is thought that Google uses a value of $\alpha=0.85$ (Google doesn’t publish this number as it is a trade secret) so that we have

$$ \kGprime = 0.85 \kG + 0.15\kHn. $$

Intuitively, this means that an Internet user will randomly follow a link from one page to another 85% of the time and will randomly jump to any other page on the Internet 15% of the time. Since the matrix $\kGprime$ is positive, the Perron-Frobenius theorem tells us that any Markov chain will converge to a unique steady-state vector that we call the PageRank vector.

The ability to access almost anything we want to know through the Internet is something we take for granted in today’s society. Without Google’s PageRank algorithm, however, the Internet would be a chaotic place indeed; imagine trying to find a useful web page among the 30 trillion available pages without it. (There are, of course, other search algorithms, but Google’s is the most widely used.) The fundamental role that Markov chains and the Perron-Frobenius theorem play in Google’s algorithm demonstrates the vast power that mathematics has to shape our society.
