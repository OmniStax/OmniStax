Variance and a new kind of function

With our understanding of symmetric matrices and variance in hand, we’ll now explore how to determine the directions in which the variance of a dataset is as large as possible and where it is as small as possible. This is part of a much larger story involving a type of function, called a quadratic form, that we’ll introduce here.

Introduction

Quadratic forms

Given a matrix $\kA$ of $N$ demeaned data points, the symmetric covariance matrix $\kC=\frac1N \kA \kA^T$ determines the variance in a particular direction

$$ \kVu = \kvecu\cdot(\kC\kvecu), $$

where $\kvecu$ is a unit vector defining the direction.

More generally, a symmetric $m\times m$ matrix $\kA$ defines a function $q:{\mathbb R}^m \to {\mathbb R}$ by

$$ q(\kvecx) = \kvecx\cdot(\kA\kvecx). $$

Notice that this expression is similar to the one we use to find the variance $\kVu$ in terms of the covariance matrix $\kC$. The only difference is that we allow $\kvecx$ to be any vector rather than requiring it to be a unit vector.

Example 7.2.2

Suppose that $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. If we write $\kvecx=\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right]$, then we have

$$ \begin{aligned} q\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right]\right) & = \left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right] \cdot \left( \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right] \right) \\ & = \left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right] \cdot \left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 + 2x_2 \\ 2x_1 + x_2 \end{array}\right] \\ & = x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 2x_1x_2 + x_2^2 \\ & = x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2. \end{aligned} $$

We may evaluate the quadratic form using some input vectors:

$$ q\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]\right) = 1, \hspace{24pt} q\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]\right) = 6, \hspace{24pt} q\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 4 \end{array}\right]\right) = 52. $$

Notice that the value of the quadratic form is a scalar.

Definition 7.2.3

If $\kA$ is a symmetric $m\times m$ matrix, the quadratic form defined by $\kA$ is the function $q_A(\kvecx) = \kvecx\cdot(\kA\kvecx)$.

Linear algebra is principally about things that are linear. However, quadratic forms, as the name implies, have a distinctly non-linear character. First, if $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}$, is a symmetric matrix, then the associated quadratic form is

$$ q_A\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right]\right) = ax_1^2 + 2bx_1x_2 + cx_2^2. $$

Notice how the variables $x_1$ and $x_2$ are multiplied together, which tells us this isn’t a linear function.

This expression assumes an especially simple form when $\kD$ is a diagonal matrix. In particular, if $\kD = \begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & c \\ \end{bmatrix}$, then $q_D\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right]\right) = ax_1^2 + cx_2^2$. This is special because there is no cross-term involving $x_1x_2$.

Remember that matrix transformations have the property that $\kT(s\kvecx) = sT(\kvecx)$. Quadratic forms behave differently:

$$ q_A(s\kvecx) = (s\kvecx)\cdot(\kA(s\kvecx)) = s^2\kvecx\cdot(\kA\kvecx)= s^2q_A(\kvecx). $$

For instance, when we multiply $\kvecx$ by the scalar 2, then $q_A(2\kvecx) = 4q_A(\kvecx)$. Also, notice that $q_A(-\kvecx) = q_A(\kvecx)$ since the scalar is squared.

Finally, evaluating a quadratic form on an eigenvector has a particularly simple form. Suppose that $\kvecx$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with associated eigenvalue $\klambda$. We then have

$$ q_A(\kvecx) = \kvecx\cdot(\kA\kvecx) = \klambda\kvecx\cdot\kvecx = \klambda \left|\kvecx\right|^2. $$

Let’s now return to our motivating question: in which direction $\kvecu$ is the variance $\kVu=\kvecu\cdot(\kC\kvecu)$ of a dataset as large as possible and in which is it as small as possible. Remembering that the vector $\kvecu$ is a unit vector, we can now state a more general form of this question: If $q_A(\kvecx)$ is a quadratic form, for which unit vectors $\kvecu$ is $q_A(\kvecu)=\kvecu\cdot(\kA\kvecu)$ as large as possible and for which is it as small as possible? Since a unit vector specifies a direction, we will often ask for the directions in which the quadratic form $q(\kvecx)$ is at its maximum or minimum value.

This activity demonstrates how the eigenvalues of $\kA$ determine the maximum and minimum values of the quadratic form $q_A(\kvecu)$ when evaluated on unit vectors and how the associated eigenvectors determine the directions in which the maximum and minimum values occur. Let’s look at another example so that this connection is clear.

Example 7.2.4

Consider the symmetric matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} -7 & -6 \\ -6 & 2 \\ \end{bmatrix}$. Because $\kA$ is symmetric, we know that it can be orthogonally diagonalized. In fact, we have $\kA=\kQ \kD \kQT$ where

$$ \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & -10 \\ \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kQ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{5} & 2/\sqrt{5} \\ -2/\sqrt{5} & 1/\sqrt{5} \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{.} $$

From this diagonalization, we know that $\klambdaone=5$ is the largest eigenvalue of $\kA$ with associated eigenvector $\kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{5} \\ -2/\sqrt{5} \end{array}\right]$ and that $\klambdatwo = -10$ is the smallest eigenvalue with associated eigenvector $\kvecutwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}2/\sqrt{5} \\ 1/\sqrt{5} \end{array}\right]$.

Let’s first study the quadratic form $q_D(\kvecu) = 5u_1^2 - 10u_2^2$ because the absence of the cross-term makes it comparatively simple. Remembering that $\kvecu$ is a unit vector, we have $u_1^2+u_2^2=1$, which means that $u_1^2 = 1-u_2^2$. Therefore,

$$ q_D(\kvecu) = 5u_1^2 - 10u_2^2 = 5(1-u_2^2)-10u_2^2 = 5 - 15u_2^2\text{.} $$

This tells us that $q_D(\kvecu)$ has a maximum value of $5$, which occurs when $u_2=0$ or in the direction $\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$.

In the same way, rewriting $u_2^2 = 1-u_1^2$ allows us to conclude that the minimum value of $q_D(\kvecu)$ is $-10$, which occurs in the direction $\left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$.

Let’s now return to the matrix $\kA$ whose quadratic form $q_A$ is related to $q_D$ because $\kA = \kQ \kD \kQT$. In particular, we have

$$ q_A(\kvecu) = \kvecu\cdot(\kA\kvecu) = \kvecu\cdot(\kQ \kD \kQT\kvecu) = (\kQT\kvecu)\cdot(\kD \kQT\kvecu) = \kvecv\cdot(\kD\kvecv) = q_D(\kvecv)\text{.} $$

In other words, we have $q_A(\kvecu) = q_D(\kvecv)$ where $\kvecv=\kQT\kvecu$. This is quite useful because it allows us to relate the values of $q_A$ to those of $q_D$, which we already understand quite well.

Now it turns out that $\kvecv$ is also a unit vector because

$$ |\kvecv|^2 = \kvecv\cdot\kvecv = (\kQT\kvecu)\cdot(\kQT\kvecu) = \kvecu\cdot(\kQ \kQT\kvecu) = \kvecu\cdot\kvecu = |\kvecu|^2 = 1\text. $$

Therefore, the maximum value of $q_A(\kvecu)$ is the same as $q_D(\kvecv)$, which we know to be $5$ and which occurs in the direction $\kvecv=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$. This means that the maximum value of $q_A(\kvecu)$ is also $5$ and that this occurs in the direction $\kvecu = \kQ\kvecv = \kQ\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{5} \\ -2/\sqrt{5} \end{array}\right]$. We now know that the maximum value of $q_A(\kvecu)$ is the largest eigenvalue $\klambdaone=5$ and that this maximum value occurs in the direction of an associated eigenvector.

In the same way, we see that the minimum value of $q_A(\kvecu)$ is the smallest eigenvalue $\klambdatwo=-10$ and that this minimum occurs in the direction of $\kvecu=\kQ\left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{r}2/\sqrt{5} \\ 1/\sqrt{5} \end{array}\right]$, an associated eigenvector.

More generally, we have

Proposition 7.2.5

Suppose that $\kA$ is a symmetric matrix, that we list its eigenvalues in decreasing order $\klambdaone \geq \klambdatwo \geq \ldots \geq \klambdam$, and that $\kvecuone,\kvecutwo,\ldots,\kvecum$ is a basis of associated eigenvectors. The maximum value of $q_A(\kvecu)$ among all unit vectors $\kvecu$ is $\klambdaone$, which occurs in the direction $\kvecuone$. Similarly, the minimum value of $q_A(\kvecu)$ is $\klambdam$, which occurs in the direction $\kvecum$.

Example 7.2.6

Suppose that $\kA$ is the symmetric matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 6 & 3 \\ 6 & 3 & 6 \\ 0 & 6 & 6 \\ \end{bmatrix}$, which may be orthogonally diagonalized as $\kA=\kQ \kD \kQT$ where

$$ \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -6 \\ \end{bmatrix}, \hspace{24pt} \kQ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & 1/3 & -2/3 \\ 2/3 & -2/3 & 1/3 \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{.} $$

We see that the maximum value of $q_A(\kvecu)$ is 12, which occurs in the direction $\left[\begin{array}{r}1/3 \\ 2/3 \\ 2/3 \end{array}\right]$, and the minimum value is -6, which occurs in the direction $\left[\begin{array}{r}2/3 \\ -2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{array}\right]$.

Example 7.2.7

Suppose we have the matrix of demeaned data points $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 2 & -3 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ that we considered in Preview Activity 7.2.1. The data points are shown in Figure 7.2.8.

Constructing the covariance matrix $\kC=\frac13~\kA \kA^T$ gives $\kC=\begin{bmatrix} 14/3 & 13/3 \\ 13/3 & 14/3 \end{bmatrix}$, which has eigenvalues $\klambdaone = 9$, with associated eigenvector $\left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right]$, and $\klambdatwo=1/3$, with associated eigenvector $\left[\begin{array}{r}-1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right]$.

Remember that the variance in a direction $\kvecu$ is $\kVu = \kvecu\cdot(\kC\kvecu) = q_C(\kvecu)$. Therefore, the variance attains a maximum value of 9 in the direction $\left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right]$ and a minimum value of 1/3 in the direction $\left[\begin{array}{r}-1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right]$. Figure 7.2.9 shows the data projected onto the lines defined by these vectors.

Remember that variance is additive, as stated in Proposition 7.1.16, which tells us that the total variance is $\kV = 9 + 1/3 = 28/3$.

We’ve been focused on finding the directions in which a quadratic form attains its maximum and minimum values, but there’s another important observation to make after this activity. Recall how we used the fact that a symmetric matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable: if $\kA=\kQ \kD \kQT$, then $q_A(\kvecu) = q_D(\kvecv)$ where $\kvecv = \kQT\kvecu$.

More generally, if we define $\kvecy = \kQT\kvecx$, we have

In other words, after a change of coordinates, the quadratic form $q_A$ can be written without cross terms. This is known as the Principal Axes Theorem.

Theorem 7.2.10 · Principal Axes Theorem

If $\kA$ is a symmetric $m\times m$ matrix with eigenvalues $\klambdaone,\klambdatwo,\ldots,\klambdam$, then the quadratic form $q_A$ can be written, after an orthogonal change of coordinates $\kvecy=\kQT\kvecx$, as

$$ q_A(\kvecx) = \klambdaone y_1^2 + \klambdatwo y_2^2 + \ldots + \klambdam y_m^2. $$

We will put this to use in the next section.

Definite symmetric matrices

While our questions about variance provide some motivation for exploring quadratic forms, these functions appear in a variety of other contexts so it’s worth spending some more time with them. For example, quadratic forms appear in multivariable calculus when describing the behavior of a function of several variables near a critical point and in physics when describing the kinetic energy of a rigid body.

The following definition will be important in this section.

Definition 7.2.11

A symmetric matrix $\kA$ is called positive definite if its associated quadratic form satisfies $q_A(\kvecx) \gt 0$ for any nonzero vector $\kvecx$. If $q_A(\kvecx) \geq 0$ for all nonzero vectors $\kvecx$, we say that $\kA$ is positive semidefinite.

Likewise, we say that $\kA$ is negative definite if $q_A(\kvecx) \lt 0$ for all nonzero vectors $\kvecx$.

Finally, $\kA$ is called indefinite if $q_A(\kvecx) \gt 0$ for some $\kvecx$ and $q_A(\kvecx) \lt 0$ for others.

As seen in this activity, it is straightforward to determine the definiteness of a diagonal matrix. For instance, if $\kD=\begin{bmatrix} 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, then

$$ q_D(\kvecx) = 7x_1^2 + 5x_2^2. $$

This shows that $q_D(\kvecx) \gt 0$ when either $x_1$ or $x_2$ is not zero so we conclude that $\kD$ is positive definite. In the same way, we see that $\kD$ is positive semidefinite if all the diagonal entries are nonnegative.

Understanding this behavior for diagonal matrices enables us to understand more general symmetric matrices. As we saw previously, the quadratic form for a symmetric matrix $\kA=\kQ \kD \kQT$ agrees with the quadratic form for the diagonal matrix $\kD$ after a change of coordinates. In particular,

$$ q_A(\kvecx) = q_D(\kvecy) $$

where $\kvecy=\kQT\kvecx$. Now the diagonal entries of $\kD$ are the eigenvalues of $\kA$ from which we conclude that $q_A(\kvecx) \gt 0$ if all the eigenvalues of $\kA$ are positive. Likewise, $q_A(\kvecx)\geq 0$ if all the eigenvalues are nonnegative.

Proposition 7.2.12

A symmetric matrix is positive definite if all its eigenvalues are positive. It is positive semidefinite if all its eigenvalues are nonnegative.

Likewise, a symmetric matrix is indefinite if some eigenvalues are positive and some are negative.

We will now apply what we’ve learned about quadratic forms to study the nature of critical points in multivariable calculus. The rest of this section assumes that the reader is familiar with ideas from multivariable calculus and can be skipped by others.

First, suppose that $f(x,y)$ is a differentiable function. We will use $f_x$ and $f_y$ to denote the partial derivatives of $f$ with respect to $x$ and $y$. Similarly, $f_{xx}$, $f_{xy}$, $f_{yx}$ and $f_{yy}$ denote the second partial derivatives. You may recall that the mixed partials, $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ are equal under a mild assumption on the function $f$. A typical question in calculus is to determine where this function has its maximum and minimum values.

Any local maximum or minimum of $f$ appears at a critical point $(x_0,y_0)$ where

$$ f_x(x_0,y_0) = 0,\hspace{24pt} f_y(x_0,y_0) = 0. $$

Near a critical point, the quadratic approximation of $f$ tells us that

$$ \begin{aligned} f(x,y)\approx f(x_0,y_0) & + \frac12 f_{xx}(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)^2 \\ & + f_{xy}(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)(y-y_0) + \frac12 f_{yy}(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0)^2. \end{aligned} $$

Near a critical point $(x_0,y_0)$ of a function $f(x,y)$, we can write

$$ f(x,y) \approx f(x_0, y_0) + q_A(\kvecw) $$

where $\kvecw = \left[\begin{array}{r}x-x_0 \\ y-y_0 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kA = \frac12 \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx}(x_0,y_0) & f_{xy}(x_0,y_0) \\ f_{yx}(x_0,y_0) & f_{yy}(x_0,y_0) \end{bmatrix}$. If $\kA$ is positive definite, then $q_A(\kvecw) \gt 0$, which tells us that

$$ f(x,y) \approx f(x_0,y_0) + q_A(\kvecw) \gt f(x_0,y_0) $$

and that the critical point $(x_0,y_0)$ is therefore a local minimum.

The matrix

$$ \kH = \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx}(x_0,y_0) & f_{xy}(x_0,y_0) \\ f_{yx}(x_0,y_0) & f_{yy}(x_0,y_0) \end{bmatrix} $$

is called the Hessian of $f$, and we see now that the eigenvalues of this symmetric matrix determine the nature of the critical point $(x_0,y_0)$. In particular, if the eigenvalues are both positive, then $q_H$ is positive definite, and the critical point is a local minimum.

This observation leads to the Second Derivative Test for multivariable functions.

Proposition 7.2.13 · Second Derivative Test

The nature of a critical point of a multivariable function is determined by the Hessian $\kH$ of the function at the critical point. If

  • $\kH$ has all positive eigenvalues, the critical point is a local minimum.
  • $\kH$ has all negative eigenvalues, the critical point is a local maximum.
  • $\kH$ has both positive and negative eigenvalues, the critical point is neither a local maximum nor minimum.

Most multivariable calculus texts assume that the reader is not familiar with linear algebra and so write the second derivative test for functions of two variables in terms of $D=\det(\kH)$. If

  • $D \gt 0$ and $f_{xx}(x_0,y_0) \gt 0$, then $(x_0, y_0)$ is a local minimum.
  • $D \gt 0$ and $f_{xx}(x_0,y_0) \lt 0$, then $(x_0, y_0)$ is a local maximum.
  • $D \lt 0$, then $(x_0,y_0)$ is neither a local maximum nor minimum.

The conditions in this version of the second derivative test are simply algebraic criteria that tell us about the definiteness of the Hessian matrix $\kH$.
