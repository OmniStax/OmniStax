Data in many dimensions

We are sometimes presented with a dataset having many data points that live in a high dimensional space. For instance, we looked at a dataset describing body fat index (BFI) in Activity 6.5.4 where each data point is six-dimensional. Developing an intuitive understanding of the data is hampered by the fact that it cannot be visualized.

This section explores a technique called principal component analysis, which enables us to reduce the dimension of a dataset so that it may be visualized or studied in a way so that interesting features more readily stand out. Our previous work with variance and the orthogonal diagonalization of symmetric matrices provides the key ideas.

Introduction

Here are some ideas we've seen previously that will be particularly useful for us in this section. Remember that the covariance matrix of a dataset is $\kC=\frac 1N \kA\kAT$ where $\kA$ is the matrix of $N$ demeaned data points.

  • When $\kvecu$ is a unit vector, the variance of the demeaned data after projecting onto the line defined by $\kvecu$ is given by the quadratic form $\kVu = \kvecu\cdot(\kC\kvecu)$.
  • In particular, if $\kvecu$ is a unit eigenvector of $\kC$ with associated eigenvalue $\klambda$, then $\kVu = \klambda$.
  • Moreover, variance is additive, as we recorded in Proposition 7.1.16: if $\kW$ is a subspace having an orthonormal basis $\kvecuone,\kvecutwo,\ldots,\kvecun$, then the variance $$ \kVW = \kVuone + \kVutwo + \ldots + \kVun\text{.} $$

Principal Component Analysis

Let's begin by looking at an example that illustrates the central theme of this technique.

This activity demonstrates how the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix can tell us when data are clustered around, or even wholly contained within, a smaller dimensional subspace. In particular, the original data is 5-dimensional, but we see that it actually lies in a 3-dimensional subspace of ${\mathbb R}^5$. Later in this section, we'll see how to use this observation to work with the data as if it were three-dimensional, an idea known as dimensional reduction.

The eigenvectors $\kvecuj$ of the covariance matrix are called principal components, and we will order them so that their associated eigenvalues decrease. Generally speaking, we hope that the first few principal components retain most of the variance, as the example in the activity demonstrates. In that example, we have the sequence of subspaces

  • $\kWone$, the 1-dimensional subspace spanned by $\kvecuone$, which retains $13/25 = 52\%$ of the total variance,
  • $\kWtwo$, the 2-dimensional subspace spanned by $\kvecuone$ and $\kvecutwo$, which retains $23/25 = 92\%$ of the variance, and
  • $\kWthree$, the 3-dimensional subspace spanned by $\kvecuone$, $\kvecutwo$, and $\kvecuthree$, which retains all of the variance.

Notice how we retain more of the total variance as we increase the dimension of the subspace onto which the data are projected. Eventually, projecting the data onto $\kWthree$ retains all the variance, which tells us the data must lie in $\kWthree$, a smaller dimensional subspace of ${\mathbb R}^5$.

In fact, these subspaces are the best possible. We know that the first principal component $\kvecuone$ is the eigenvector of $\kC$ associated to the largest eigenvalue. This means that the variance is as large as possible in the $\kvecuone$ direction. In other words, projecting onto any other line will retain a smaller amount of variance. Similarly, projecting onto any other 2-dimensional subspace besides $\kWtwo$ will retain less variance than projecting onto $\kWtwo$. The principal components have the wonderful ability to pick out the best possible subspaces to retain as much variance as possible.

Of course, this is a contrived example. Typically, the presence of noise in a dataset means that we do not expect all the points to be wholly contained in a smaller dimensional subspace. In fact, the 2-dimensional subspace $\kWtwo$ retains $92\%$ of the variance. Depending on the situation, we may want to write off the remaining $8\%$ of the variance as noise in exchange for the convenience of working with a smaller dimensional subspace. As we'll see later, we will seek a balance using a number of principal components large enough to retain most of the variance but small enough to be easy to work with.

This example is a more realistic illustration of principal component analysis. The plot of the 3-dimensional data appears to show that the data lies close to a plane, and the principal components will identify this plane. Starting with the $3\times100$ matrix of demeaned data $\kA$, we construct the covariance matrix $\kC=\frac{1}{100} ~\kA\kAT$ and study its eigenvalues. Notice that the first two principal components account for more than 98% of the variance, which means we can expect the points to lie close to $\kWtwo$, the two-dimensional subspace spanned by $\kvecuone$ and $\kvecutwo$.

Since $\kWtwo$ is a subspace of ${\mathbb R}^3$, projecting the data points onto $\kWtwo$ gives a list of 100 points in ${\mathbb R}^3$. In order to visualize them more easily, we instead consider the coordinates of the projections in the basis defined by $\kvecuone$ and $\kvecutwo$. For instance, we know that the projection of a data point $\kvecx$ is

$$ \kvecxhat = (\kvecuone\cdot\kvecx)\kvecuone + (\kvecutwo\cdot\kvecx)\kvecutwo, $$

which is a three-dimensional vector. Instead, we can record the coordinates $\left[\begin{array}{r}\kvecuone\cdot\kvecx \\ \kvecutwo\cdot\kvecx \end{array}\right]$ and plot them in the two-dimensional coordinate plane, as illustrated in Figure 7.3.1.

If we form the matrix $\kQ=\begin{bmatrix}\kvecuone & \kvecutwo \end{bmatrix}$, then we have

$$ \kQT\kvecx = \left[\begin{array}{r}\kvecuone\cdot\kvecx \\ \kvecutwo\cdot\kvecx \end{array}\right]. $$

This means that the columns of $\kQT\kA$ represent the coordinates of the projected points, which may now be plotted in the plane.

In this plot, the first coordinate, represented by the horizontal coordinate, represents the projection of a data point onto the line defined by $\kvecuone$ while the second coordinate represents the projection onto the line defined by $\kvecutwo$. Since $\kvecuone$ is the first principal component, the variance in the $\kvecuone$ direction is greater than the variance in the $\kvecutwo$ direction. For this reason, the plot will be more spread out in the horizontal direction than in the vertical.

Using Principal Component Analysis

Now that we've explored the ideas behind principal component analysis, we will look at a few examples that illustrate its use.

This activity demonstrates how principal component analysis enables us to extract information from a dataset that may not be easily obtained otherwise. As in our previous example, we see that the data points lie quite close to a two-dimensional subspace of ${\mathbb R}^{17}$. In fact, $\kWtwo$, the subspace spanned by the first two principal components, accounts for more than 96% of the variance. More importantly, when we project the data onto $\kWtwo$, it becomes apparent that Northern Ireland is fundamentally different from the other three nations.

With some additional thought, we can determine more specific ways in which Northern Ireland is different. On the $2$-dimensional plot, Northern Ireland lies far to the right compared to the other three nations. Since the data has been demeaned, the origin $(0,0)$ in this plot corresponds to the average of the four nations. The coordinates of the point representing Northern Ireland are about $(477, 59)$, meaning that the projected data point differs from the mean by about $477\kvecuone+59\kvecutwo$.

Let's just focus on the contribution from $\kvecuone$. We see that the ninth component of $\kvecuone$, the one that describes Fresh Fruit, is about $-0.63$. This means that the ninth component of $477\kvecuone$ differs from the mean by about $477(-0.63) = -300$ grams per person per week. So roughly speaking, people in Northern Ireland are eating about 300 fewer grams of Fresh Fruit than the average across the four nations. This is borne out by looking at the original data, which show that the consumption of Fresh Fruit in Northern Ireland is significantly less than the other nations. Examing the other components of $\kvecuone$ shows other ways in which Northern Ireland differs from the other three nations.
