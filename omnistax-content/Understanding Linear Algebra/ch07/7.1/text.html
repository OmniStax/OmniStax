A special class of matrices

In this section, we will revisit the theory of eigenvalues and eigenvectors for the special class of matrices that are symmetric, meaning that the matrix equals its transpose. This understanding of symmetric matrices will enable us to form singular value decompositions later in the chapter. We’ll also begin studying variance in this section as it provides an important context that motivates some of our later work.

Introduction

To begin, remember that if $\kA$ is a square matrix, we say that $\kvecv$ is an eigenvector of $\kA$ with associated eigenvalue $\klambda$ if $\kA\kvecv=\klambda\kvecv$. In other words, for these special vectors, the operation of matrix multiplication simplifies to scalar multiplication.

The preview activity asks us to compare the matrix transformations defined by two matrices, a diagonal matrix $\kD$ and a matrix $\kA$ whose eigenvectors are given to us. The transformation defined by $\kD$ stretches horizontally by a factor of 3 and reflects in the horizontal axis, as shown in Figure 7.1.2

By contrast, the transformation defined by $\kA$ stretches the plane by a factor of 3 in the direction of $\kvecvone$ and reflects in the line defined by $\kvecvone$, as seen in Figure 7.1.3.

In this way, we see that the matrix transformations defined by these two matrices are equivalent after a $45^\circ$ rotation. This notion of equivalence is what we called similarity in Section 4.3. There we considered a square $m\times m$ matrix $\kA$ that provided enough eigenvectors to form a basis of ${\mathbb R}^m$. For example, suppose we can construct a basis for ${\mathbb R}^m$ using eigenvectors $\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo,\ldots,\kvecv_m$ having associated eigenvalues $\klambdaone,\klambdatwo,\ldots,\klambdam$. Forming the matrices,

$$ \kP = \begin{bmatrix} \kvecvone&\kvecvtwo&\ldots&\kvecv_m \end{bmatrix}, \hspace{24pt} \kD = \begin{bmatrix} \klambdaone & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & \klambdatwo & \ldots & 0 \\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \ldots & \klambdam \end{bmatrix}, $$

enables us to write $\kA = \kP\kD\kPinv$. This is what it means for $\kA$ to be diagonalizable.

For the example in the preview activity, we are led to form

$$ \kP = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \hspace{24pt} \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & - 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

which tells us that $\kA=\kP\kD\kPinv = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Notice that the matrix $\kA$ has eigenvectors $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ that not only form a basis for ${\mathbb R}^2$ but, in fact, form an orthogonal basis for ${\mathbb R}^2$. Given the prominent role played by orthogonal bases in the last chapter, we would like to understand what conditions on a matrix enable us to form an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors.

Symmetric matrices and orthogonal diagonalization

Let’s begin by looking at some examples in the next activity.

The examples in this activity illustrate a range of possibilities. First, a matrix may have complex eigenvalues, in which case it will not be diagonalizable. Second, even if all the eigenvalues are real, there may not be a basis of eigenvalues if the dimension of one of the eigenspaces is less than the algebraic multiplicity of the associated eigenvalue.

We are interested in matrices for which there is an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors. When this happens, we can create an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors by scaling each eigenvector in the basis so that its length is 1. Putting these orthonormal vectors into a matrix $\kQ$ produces an orthogonal matrix, which means that $\kQT=\kQinv$. We then have

$$ \kA = \kQ\kD\kQinv = \kQ\kD\kQT. $$

In this case, we say that $\kA$ is orthogonally diagonalizable.

Definition 7.1.4

If there is an orthonormal basis of ${\mathbb R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of the matrix $\kA$, we say that $\kA$ is orthogonally diagonalizable. In particular, we can write $\kA=\kQ\kD\kQT$ where $\kQ$ is an orthogonal matrix.

When $\kA$ is orthogonally diagonalizable, notice that

$$ \kAT=(\kQ\kD\kQT)^T = (\kQT)^T\kDT\kQT = \kQ\kD\kQT = \kA. $$

That is, when $\kA$ is orthogonally diagonalizable, $\kA=\kAT$ and we say that $\kA$ is symmetric.

Definition 7.1.5

A symmetric matrix $\kA$ is one for which $\kA=\kAT$.

Example 7.1.6

Consider the matrix $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 36 \\ 36 & -23 \end{bmatrix}$, which has eigenvectors $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}4 \\ 3 \end{array}\right]$, with associated eigenvalue $\klambdaone=25$, and $\kvecvtwo=\left[\begin{array}{r}3 \\ -4 \end{array}\right]$, with associated eigenvalue $\klambdatwo=-50$. Notice that $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ are orthogonal so we can form an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors:

$$ \kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}4/5 \\ 3/5 \end{array}\right], \hspace{24pt} \kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}3/5 \\ -4/5 \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

In this way, we construct the matrices

$$ \kQ = \begin{bmatrix} 4/5 & 3/5 \\ 3/5 & -4/5 \\ \end{bmatrix}, \hspace{24pt} \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 0 \\ 0 & -50 \end{bmatrix} $$

and note that $\kA = \kQ\kD\kQT$. Notice also that, as expected, $\kA$ is symmetric; that is, $\kA=\kAT$.

Example 7.1.7

If $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$, then there is an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors $\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ with eigenvalues $\klambdaone=3$ and $\klambdatwo=-1$. Using these eigenvectors, we form the orthogonal matrix $\kQ$ consisting of eigenvectors and the diagonal matrix $\kD$, where

$$ \kQ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & - 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Then we have $\kA = \kQ\kD\kQT$.

Notice that the matrix transformation represented by $\kQ$ is a $45^\circ$ rotation while that represented by $\kQT=\kQinv$ is a $-45^\circ$ rotation. Therefore, if we multiply a vector $\kvecx$ by $\kA$, we can decompose the multiplication as

$$ \kA\kvecx = \kQ(\kD(\kQT\kvecx)). $$

That is, we first rotate $\kvecx$ by $-45^\circ$, then apply the diagonal matrix $\kD$, which stretches and reflects, and finally rotate by $45^\circ$. We may visualize this factorization as in Figure 7.1.8.

In fact, a similar picture holds any time the matrix $\kA$ is orthogonally diagonalizable.

We have seen that a matrix that is orthogonally diagonalizable must be symmetric. In fact, it turns out that any symmetric matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable. We record this fact in the next theorem.

Theorem 7.1.9 · The Spectral Theorem

The matrix $\kA$ is orthogonally diagonalizable if and only if $\kA$ is symmetric.

As the examples in Activity 7.1.3 illustrate, the Spectral Theorem implies a number of things. Namely, if $\kA$ is a symmetric $m\times m$ matrix, then

  • the eigenvalues of $\kA$ are real.
  • there is a basis of ${\mathbb R}^m$ consisting of eigenvectors.
  • two eigenvectors that are associated to different eigenvalues are orthogonal.

We won’t justify the first two facts here since that would take us rather far afield. However, it will be helpful to explain the third fact. To begin, notice the following:

$$ \kvecv\cdot(\kA\kvecw) = \kvecv^T\kA\kvecw = (\kAT\kvecv)^T\kvecw = (\kAT\kvecv)\cdot \kvecw. $$

This is a useful fact that we’ll employ quite a bit in the future so let’s summarize it in the following proposition.

Proposition 7.1.10

For any matrix $\kA$, we have

$$ \kvecv\cdot(\kA\kvecw) = (\kAT\kvecv)\cdot\kvecw. $$

In particular, if $\kA$ is symmetric, then

$$ \kvecv\cdot(\kA\kvecw) = (\kA\kvecv)\cdot\kvecw. $$

Example 7.1.11

Suppose a symmetric matrix $\kA$ has eigenvectors $\kvecvone$, with associated eigenvalue $\klambdaone=3$, and $\kvecvtwo$, with associated eigenvalue $\klambdatwo = 10$. Notice that

$$ \begin{aligned} (\kA\kvecvone)\cdot\kvecvtwo & = 3\kvecvone\cdot\kvecvtwo \\ \kvecvone\cdot(\kA\kvecvtwo) & = 10\kvecvone\cdot\kvecvtwo. \end{aligned} $$

Since $(\kA\kvecvone)\cdot\kvecvtwo = \kvecvone\cdot(\kA\kvecvtwo)$ by Proposition 7.1.10, we have

$$ 3\kvecvone\cdot\kvecvtwo = 10 \kvecvone\cdot\kvecvtwo, $$

which can only happen if $\kvecvone\cdot\kvecvtwo = 0$. Therefore, $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ are orthogonal.

More generally, the same argument shows that two eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix associated to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Variance

Many of the ideas we’ll encounter in this chapter, such as orthogonal diagonalizations, can be applied to the study of data. In fact, it can be useful to understand these applications because they provide an important context in which mathematical ideas have a more concrete meaning and their motivation appears more clearly. For that reason, we will now introduce the statistical concept of variance as a way to gain insight into the significance of orthogonal diagonalizations.

Given a set of data points, their variance measures how spread out the points are. The next activity looks at some examples.

Notice that variance enjoys an additivity property. Consider, for instance, the situation where our data points are two-dimensional and suppose that the demeaned points are $\kvecdjtilde=\left[\begin{array}{r}\widetilde{x}_j \\ \widetilde{y}_j \end{array}\right]$. We have

$$ |\kvecdjtilde|^2 = \widetilde{x}_j^2 + \widetilde{y}_j^2. $$

$$ \kVuone = \frac1N\sum_j ~|(\kvecdjtilde\cdot\kvecuone)~\kvecuone|^2 = \frac1N\sum_j~(\kvecdjtilde\cdot\kvecuone)^2. $$

For each of our demeaned data points, the Projection Formula tells us that

$$ \kvecdjtilde = (\kvecdjtilde\cdot\kvecuone)~\kvecuone + (\kvecdjtilde\cdot\kvecutwo)~\kvecutwo. $$

Proposition 7.1.16 · Additivity of Variance

If $\kW$ is a subspace with orthonormal basis $\kvecuone$,$\kvecutwo$,$\ldots$, $\kvecun$, then the variance of the points projected onto $\kW$ is the sum of the variances in the $\kvecuj$ directions:

$$ \kVW = \kVuone + \kVutwo + \ldots + \kVun. $$

The next activity demonstrates a more efficient way to find the variance $\kVu$ in a particular direction and connects our discussion of variance with symmetric matrices.

This activity introduced the covariance matrix of a dataset, which is defined to be $\kC=\frac1N~\kA\kAT$ where $\kA$ is the matrix of demeaned data points. Notice that

$$ \kCT = \frac1N~(\kA\kAT)^T = \frac1N~\kA\kAT = \kC, $$

which tells us that $\kC$ is symmetric. In particular, we know that it is orthogonally diagonalizable, an observation that will play an important role in the future.

This activity also demonstrates the significance of the covariance matrix, which is recorded in the following proposition.

Proposition 7.1.17

If $\kC$ is the covariance matrix associated to a demeaned dataset and $\kvecu$ is a unit vector, then the variance of the demeaned points projected onto the line defined by $\kvecu$ is

$$ \kVu = \kvecu\cdot \kC\kvecu. $$

Our goal in the future will be to find directions $\kvecu$ where the variance is as large as possible and directions where it is as small as possible. The next activity demonstrates why this is useful.

This activity illustrates how variance can identify a line along which the data are concentrated. When the data primarily lie along a line defined by a vector $\kvecuone$, then the variance in that direction will be large while the variance in an orthogonal direction $\kvecutwo$ will be small.

Remember that variance is additive, according to Proposition 7.1.16, so that if $\kvecuone$ and $\kvecutwo$ are orthogonal unit vectors, then the total variance is

$$ \kV = \kVuone + \kVutwo. $$

Therefore, if we choose $\kvecuone$ to be the direction where $\kVuone$ is a maximum, then $\kVutwo$ will be a minimum.

In the next section, we will use an orthogonal diagonalization of the covariance matrix $\kC$ to find the directions having the greatest and smallest variances. In this way, we will be able to determine when data are concentrated along a line or subspace.
