Beyond symmetric matrices

The Spectral Theorem has motivated the past few sections. In particular, we applied the fact that symmetric matrices can be orthogonally diagonalized to simplify quadratic forms, which enabled us to use principal component analysis to reduce the dimension of a dataset. But what can we do with matrices that are not symmetric or even square? For instance, the following matrices are not diagonalizable, much less orthogonally so:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \hspace{24pt} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

In this section, we will develop a description of matrices called the singular value decomposition that is, in many ways, analogous to an orthogonal diagonalization. For example, we have seen that any symmetric matrix can be written in the form $\kQ\kD\kQT$ where $\kQ$ is an orthogonal matrix and $\kD$ is diagonal. A singular value decomposition will have the form $\kU\kSigma\kVT$ where $\kU$ and $\kV$ are orthogonal and $\kSigma$ is diagonal. Most notably, we will see that every matrix has a singular value decomposition whether it’s symmetric or not.

Introduction

Finding singular value decompositions

We will begin by explaining what a singular value decomposition is and how we can find one for a given matrix $\kA$.

Recall how the orthogonal diagonalization of a symmetric matrix is formed: if $\kA$ is symmetric, we write $\kA = \kQ\kD\kQT$ where the diagonal entries of $\kD$ are the eigenvalues of $\kA$ and the columns of $\kQ$ are the associated eigenvectors. Moreover, the eigenvalues are related to the maximum and minimum values of the associated quadratic form $q_{\kA}(\mathbf u)$ among all unit vectors.

A general matrix, particularly a matrix that is not square, may not have eigenvalues and eigenvectors, but we can discover analogous features, called singular values and singular vectors, by studying a function somewhat similar to a quadratic form. More specifically, any matrix $\kA$ defines a function

$$ l_{\kA}(\kvecx) = |\kA\kvecx|, $$

which measures the length of $\kA\kvecx$. For example, the diagonal matrix $\kD=\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ gives the function $l_{\kD}(\kvecx) = \sqrt{9x_1^2 + 4x_2^2}$. The presence of the square root means that this function is not a quadratic form. We can, however, define the singular values and vectors by looking for the maximum and minimum of this function $l_{\kA}(\mathbf u)$ among all unit vectors $\mathbf u$.

While $l_{\kA}(\kvecx)$ is not itself a quadratic form, it becomes one if we square it:

$$ \left(l_{\kA}(\kvecx)\right)^2 = |\kA\kvecx|^2 = (\kA\kvecx)\cdot(\kA\kvecx) = \kvecx\cdot(\kAT\kA\kvecx)=q_{A^TA}(\kvecx)\text{.} $$

We call $\kG=\kAT\kA$, the Gram matrix associated to $\kA$ and note that

$$ l_{\kA}(\kvecx) = \sqrt{q_{\kG}(\kvecx)}\text{.} $$

This is important in the next activity, which introduces singular values and singular vectors.

As this activity shows, the singular values of $\kA$ are the maximum and minimum values of $l_{\kA}(\kvecx)=|\kA\kvecx|$ among all unit vectors and the right singular vectors $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ are the directions in which they occur. The key to finding the singular values and vectors is to utilize the Gram matrix $\kG$ and its associated quadratic form $q_{\kG}(\kvecx)$. We will illustrate with some more examples.

Example 7.4.2

We will find a singular value decomposition of the matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Notice that this matrix is not symmetric so it cannot be orthogonally diagonalized.

We begin by constructing the Gram matrix $\kG = \kAT\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 8 \\ \end{bmatrix}$. Since $\kG$ is symmetric, it can be orthogonally diagonalized with

$$ \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kQ = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{.} $$

We now know that the maximum value of the quadratic form $q_{\kG}(\kvecx)$ is 8, which occurs in the direction $\left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. Since $l_{\kA}(\kvecx) = \sqrt{q_{\kG}(\kvecx)}$, this tells us that the maximum value of $l_{\kA}(\kvecx)$, the first singular value, is $\ksigmaone=\sqrt{8}$ and that this occurs in the direction of the first right singular vector $\kvecvone=\left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$.

In the same way, we also know that the second singular value $\ksigmatwo=\sqrt{2}$ with associated right singular vector $\kvecvtwo=\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$.

The first left singular vector $\kvecuone$ is defined by $\kA\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] = \ksigmaone\kvecuone$. Because $\ksigmaone = \sqrt{8}$, we have $\kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right]$. Notice that $\kvecuone$ is a unit vector because $\ksigmaone = |\kA\kvecvone|$.

In the same way, the second left singular vector is defined by $\kA\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ -1 \end{array}\right] = \ksigmatwo\kvecutwo$, which gives us $\kvecutwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{2} \\ -1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right]$.

We then construct

$$ \begin{aligned} \kU & {}={} \begin{bmatrix} \kvecuone & \kvecutwo \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ \end{bmatrix} \\ \kSigma & {}={} \begin{bmatrix} \ksigmaone & 0 \\ 0 & \ksigmatwo \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{8} & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{2} \\ \end{bmatrix} \\ \kV & {}={} \begin{bmatrix} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} $$

We now have $\kA\kV=\kU\kSigma$ because

$$ \kA\kV = \begin{bmatrix} \kA\kvecvone & \kA\kvecvtwo \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \ksigmaone\kvecuone & \ksigmatwo\kvecutwo \end{bmatrix} = \kSigma\kU\text{.} $$

Because the right singular vectors, the columns of $\kV$, are eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $\kG$, they form an orthonormal basis, which means that $\kV$ is orthogonal. Therefore, we have $(\kA\kV)\kVT = \kA = \kU\kSigma\kVT$. This gives the singular value decomposition

$$ \kA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{8} & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{2} \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}^T = \kU\kSigma\kVT\text{.} $$

To summarize, we find a singular value decomposition of a matrix $\kA$ in the following way:

  • Construct the Gram matrix $\kG=\kAT\kA$ and find an orthogonal diagonalization to obtain eigenvalues $\klambdai$ and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

  • The singular values of $\kA$ are the squares roots of eigenvalues $\klambdai$ of $\kG$; that is, $\ksigmai = \sqrt{\klambdai}$. By convention, the singular values are listed in decreasing order: $\ksigmaone \geq \ksigmatwo \geq \ldots$. The right singular vectors $\kvecvi$ are the associated eigenvectors of $\kG$.

  • The left singular vectors $\kvecui$ are found by $\kA\kvecvi = \ksigmai\kvecui$. Because $\ksigmai=|\kA\kvecvi|$, we know that $\kvecui$ will be a unit vector.

    In fact, the left singular vectors will also form an orthonormal basis. To see this, suppose that the associated singular values are nonzero. We then have:

since the right singular vectors are orthogonal.

Example 7.4.3

Let’s find a singular value decomposition for the symmetric matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The associated Gram matrix is

$$ \kG = \kAT\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{,} $$

which has an orthogonal diagonalization with

$$ \kD = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kQ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{.} $$

This gives singular values and vectors

$$ \begin{aligned} \ksigmaone = 3, \hspace{24pt}& \kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right], & \kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right] \\ \ksigmatwo = 1, \hspace{24pt}& \kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}1/\sqrt{2} \\ -1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right], & \kvecutwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}-1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} $$

and the singular value decomposition $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$ where

$$ \kU = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kSigma = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kV = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}. $$

This example is special because $\kA$ is symmetric. With a little thought, it’s possible to relate this singular value decomposition to an orthogonal diagonalization of $\kA$ using the fact that $\kG=\kAT\kA = \kA^2$.

Example 7.4.4

We will find a singular value decomposition of the matrix $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 & 1 \\ -4 & -8 & -8 \\ \end{bmatrix}$.

Finding an orthogonal diagonalization of $\kG=\kAT\kA$ gives

$$ \kD=\begin{bmatrix} 144 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 9 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kQ=\begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -2/3 & 1/3 \\ 2/3 & 1/3 & -2/3 \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{,} $$

which gives singular values $\ksigmaone=\sqrt{144}=12$, $\ksigmatwo = \sqrt{9}= 3$, and $\ksigmathree = 0$. The right singular vectors $\kvecvi$ appear as the columns of $\kQ$ so that $\kV = \kQ$.

We now find

$$ \begin{aligned} \kA\kvecvone = \left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ -12 \end{array}\right] = 12\kvecuone, \hspace{24pt} & \kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ -1 \end{array}\right] \\ \kA\kvecvtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}3 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] = 3\kvecuone, \hspace{24pt} & \kvecuone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] \\ \kA\kvecvthree = \left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} $$

Notice that it’s not possible to find a third left singular vector since $\kA\kvecvthree={\mathbf 0}$. We therefore form the matrices

$$ \kU = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kSigma = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix},\hspace{24pt} \kV=\begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -2/3 & 1/3 \\ 2/3 & 1/3 & -2/3 \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{,} $$

which gives the singular value decomposition $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$.

Notice that $\kU$ is a $2\times2$ orthogonal matrix because $\kA$ has two rows, and $\kV$ is a $3\times3$ orthogonal matrix because $\kA$ has three columns.

As we’ll see in the next section, some additional work may be needed to construct the left singular vectors $\kvecuj$ if more of the singular values are zero, but we won’t worry about that now. For the time being, let’s record our work in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.4.5 · The singular value decomposition

An $m\times n$ matrix $\kA$ may be written as $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$ where $\kU$ is an orthogonal $m\times m$ matrix, $\kV$ is an orthogonal $n\times n$ matrix, and $\kSigma$ is an $m\times n$ matrix whose entries are zero except for the singular values of $\kA$ which appear in decreasing order on the diagonal.

Notice that a singular value decomposition of $\kA$ gives us a singular value decomposition of $\kAT$. More specifically, if $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$, then

$$ \kAT = (\kU\kSigma\kVT)^T = \kV\kSigmaT\kUT. $$
Proposition 7.4.6

If $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$, then $\kAT = \kV\kSigmaT\kUT$. In other words, $\kA$ and $\kAT$ share the same singular values, and the left singular vectors of $\kA$ are the right singular vectors of $\kAT$ and vice-versa.

As we said earlier, a singular value decomposition should be thought of a generalization of an orthogonal diagonalization. For instance, the Spectral Theorem tells us that a symmetric matrix can be written as $\kQ\kD\kQT$. Many matrices, however, are not symmetric and so they are not orthogonally diagonalizable. However, every matrix has a singular value decomposition $\kU\kSigma\kVT$. The price of this generalization is that we usually have two sets of singular vectors that form the orthogonal matrices $\kU$ and $\kV$ whereas a symmetric matrix has a single set of eignevectors that form the orthogonal matrix $\kQ$.

The structure of singular value decompositions

Now that we have an understanding of what a singular value decomposition is and how to construct it, let’s explore the ways in which a singular value decomposition reveals the underlying structure of the matrix. As we’ll see, the matrices $\kU$ and $\kV$ in a singular value decomposition provide convenient bases for some important subspaces, such as the column and null spaces of the matrix. This observation will provide the key to some of our uses of these decompositions in the next section.

This activity shows how a singular value decomposition of a matrix encodes important information about its null and column spaces. More specifically, the left and right singular vectors provide orthonormal bases for $\kNul(\kA)$ and $\kCol(\kA)$. This is one of the reasons that singular value decompositions are so useful.

Example 7.4.7

Suppose we have a singular value decomposition $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$ where $\kSigma = \begin{bmatrix} \ksigmaone & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \ksigmatwo & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \ksigmathree & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}$. This means that $\kA$ has four rows and five columns just as $\kSigma$ does.

As in the activity, if $\kvecx = c_1 \kvecvone + c_2\kvecvtwo + \ldots + c_5{\mathbf v}_5$, we have

$$ \kA\kvecx = \ksigmaone c_1\kvecuone + \ksigmatwo c_2\kvecutwo + \ksigmathree c_3\kvecuthree\text{.} $$

If $\kvecb$ is in $\kCol(\kA)$, then $\kvecb$ must have the form

$$ \kvecb = \ksigmaone c_1\kvecuone + \ksigmatwo c_2\kvecutwo + \ksigmathree c_3\kvecuthree\text{,} $$

which says that $\kvecb$ is a linear combination of $\kvecuone$, $\kvecutwo$, and $\kvecuthree$. These three vectors therefore form a basis for $\kCol(\kA)$. In fact, since they are columns in the orthogonal matrix $\kU$, they form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kA)$.

Remembering that $\operatorname{rank}(\kA)=\dim\kCol(\kA)$, we see that $\operatorname{rank}(\kA) = 3$, which results from the three nonzero singular values. In general, the rank $r$ of a matrix $\kA$ equals the number of nonzero singular values, and $\kvecuone, \kvecutwo, \ldots,\kvecur$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kA)$.

Moreover, if $\kvecx = c_1 \kvecvone + c_2\kvecvtwo + \ldots + c_5{\mathbf v}_5$ satisfies $\kA\kvecx = {\mathbf 0}$, then

$$ \kA\kvecx = \ksigmaone c_1\kvecuone + \ksigmatwo c_2\kvecutwo + \ksigmathree c_3\kvecuthree={\mathbf 0}\text{,} $$

which implies that $c_1=0$, $c_2=0$, and $c_3=0$. Therefore, $\kvecx = c_4{\mathbf v}_4+c_5{\mathbf v}_5$ so ${\mathbf v}_4$ and ${\mathbf v}_5$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kNul(\kA)$.

More generally, if $\kA$ is an $m\times n$ matrix and if $\operatorname{rank}(\kA) = r$, the last $n-r$ right singular vectors form an orthonormal basis for $\kNul(\kA)$.

Generally speaking, if the rank of an $m\times n$ matrix $\kA$ is $r$, then there are $r$ nonzero singular values and $\kSigma$ has the form

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \ksigmaone & \ldots & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & \ldots & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & \ldots & \ksigmar & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & \ldots & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \ldots & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix}, $$

The first $r$ columns of $\kU$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kA)$:

$$ \kU = \left[ \underbrace{\kvecuone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecur}_{\kCol(\kA)} \hspace{3pt} \kvecurone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecum \right] $$

and the last $n-r$ columns of $\kV$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kNul(\kA)$:

$$ \kV = \left[ \kvecvone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecvr\hspace{3pt} \underbrace{\kvecvrone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecvn}_{\kNul(\kA)} \right] $$

Remember that Proposition 7.4.6 says that $\kA$ and its transpose $\kAT$ share the same singular values. Since the rank of a matrix equals its number of nonzero singular values, this means that $\operatorname{rank}(\kA)=\operatorname{rank}(\kAT)$, a fact that we cited back in Section 6.2.

Proposition 7.4.8

For any matrix $\kA$,

$$ \operatorname{rank}(\kA) = \operatorname{rank}(\kAT)\text{.} $$

If we have a singular value decomposition of an $m\times n$ matrix $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$, Proposition 7.4.6 also tells us that the left singular vectors of $\kA$ are the right singular vectors of $\kAT$. Therefore, $\kU$ is the $m\times m$ matrix whose columns are the right singular vectors of $\kAT$. This means that the last $m-r$ vectors form an orthonormal basis for $\kNul(\kAT)$. Therefore, the columns of $\kU$ provide orthonormal bases for $\kCol(\kA)$ and $\kNul(\kAT)$:

$$ \kU = \left[ \underbrace{\kvecuone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecur}_{\kCol(\kA)} \hspace{3pt} \underbrace{\kvecurone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecum}_{\kNul(\kAT)} \right]\text{.} $$

This reflects the familiar fact that $\kNul(\kAT)$ is the orthogonal complement of $\kCol(\kA)$.

In the same way, $\kV$ is the $n\times n$ matrix whose columns are the left singular vectors of $\kAT$, which means that the first $r$ vectors form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kAT)$. Because the columns of $\kAT$ are the rows of $\kA$, this subspace is sometimes called the row space of $\kA$ and denoted $\kRow(\kA)$. While we have yet to have an occasion to use $\kRow(\kA)$, there are times when it is important to have an orthonormal basis for it, and a singular value decomposition provides just that. To summarize, the columns of $\kV$ provide orthonormal bases for $\kCol(\kAT)$ and $\kNul(\kA)$:

$$ \kV = \left[ \underbrace{\kvecvone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecvr}_{\kCol(\kAT)}\hspace{3pt} \underbrace{\kvecvrone ~ \ldots ~ \kvecvn}_{\kNul(\kA)} \right] $$

Considered altogether, the subspaces $\kCol(\kA)$, $\kNul(\kA)$, $\kCol(\kAT)$, and $\kNul(\kAT)$ are called the four fundamental subspaces associated to $\kA$. In addition to telling us the rank of a matrix, a singular value decomposition gives us orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces.

Theorem 7.4.9

Suppose $\kA$ is an $m\times n$ matrix having a singular value decomposition $\kA=\kU\kSigma\kVT$. Then

  • $r=\operatorname{rank}(\kA)$ is the number of nonzero singular values.

  • The columns $\kvecuone,\kvecutwo,\ldots,\kvecur$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kA)$.

  • The columns $\kvecurone,\ldots,\kvecum$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kNul(\kAT)$.

  • The columns $\kvecvone,\kvecvtwo,\ldots,\kvecvr$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kAT)$.

  • The columns $\kvecvrone,\ldots,\kvecvn$ form an orthonormal basis for $\kNul(\kA)$.

When we previously outlined a procedure for finding a singular decomposition of an $m\times n$ matrix $\kA$, we found the left singular vectors $\kvecuj$ using the expression $\kA\kvecvj = \ksigmaj\kvecuj$. This produces left singular vectors $\kvecuone, \kvecutwo,\ldots,\kvecur$, where $r=\operatorname{rank}(\kA)$. If $r< m$, however, we still need to find the left singular vectors $\kvecurone,\ldots,\kvecum$. Theorem 7.4.9 tells us how to do that: because those vectors form an orthonormal basis for $\kNul(\kAT)$, we can find them by solving $\kAT\kvecx = {\mathbf 0}$ to obtain a basis for $\kNul(\kAT)$ and applying the Gram-Schmidt algorithm.

We won’t worry about this issue too much, however, as we will frequently use software to find singular value decompositions for us.

Reduced singular value decompositions

As we’ll see in the next section, there are times when it is helpful to express a singular value decomposition in a slightly different form.

We call this a reduced singular value decomposition.

Proposition 7.4.10 · Reduced singular value decomposition

If $\kA$ is an $m\times n$ matrix having rank $r$, then $\kA=\kUr \kSigmar \kVr^T$ where

  • $\kUr$ is an $m\times r$ matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kA)$,

  • $\kSigmar=\begin{bmatrix} \ksigmaone & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & \ksigmatwo & \ldots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \ksigmar \\ \end{bmatrix}$ is an $r\times r$ diagonal, invertible matrix, and

  • $\kVr$ is an $n\times r$ matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for $\kCol(\kAT)$.

Example 7.4.11

In Example 7.4.4, we found the singular value decomposition

$$ \kA=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 & 1 \\ -4 & -8 & -8 \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 2/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -2/3 & 1/3 \\ 2/3 & 1/3 & -2/3 \\ \end{bmatrix}^T\text{.} $$

Since there are two nonzero singular values, $\operatorname{rank}(\kA) =2$ so that the reduced singular value decomposition is

$$ \kA=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 & 1 \\ -4 & -8 & -8 \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -2/3 \\ 2/3 & 1/3 \\ \end{bmatrix}^T\text{.} $$
