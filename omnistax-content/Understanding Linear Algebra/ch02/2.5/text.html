Functions built from matrices

The past few sections introduced us to matrix-vector multiplication as a means of thinking geometrically about the solutions to a linear system. In particular, we rewrote a linear system as a matrix equation $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$ and developed the concepts of span and linear independence in response to our two fundamental questions.

In this section, we will explore how matrix-vector multiplication defines certain types of functions, which we call matrix transformations, similar to those encountered in previous algebra courses. In particular, we will develop some algebraic tools for thinking about matrix transformations and look at some motivating examples. In the next section, we will see how matrix transformations describe important geometric operations and how they are used in computer animation.

Introduction

Matrix transformations

In the preview activity, we considered familiar linear functions of a single variable, such as $g(x) = 2x$. We construct a function like this by choosing a number $m$; when given an input $x$, the output $g(x) = mx$ is formed by multiplying $x$ by $m$.

In this section, we will consider functions whose inputs are vectors and whose outputs are vectors defined through matrix-vector multiplication. That is, if $\kA$ is a matrix and $\kvecx$ is a vector, the function $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ forms the product $\kA\kvecx$ as its output. Such a function is called a matrix transformation.

Definition 2.5.3

The matrix transformation associated to the matrix $\kA$ is the function that assigns to the vector $\kvecx$ the vector $\kA\kvecx$; that is, $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$.

Example 2.5.4

The matrix $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ defines a matrix transformation $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ in the following way:

$$ \kT\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right]\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{c}3x_1 - 2x_2 \\ x_1 + 2x_2 \\ 3x_2 \end{array}\right]. $$

Notice that the input to $\kT$ is a two-dimensional vector $\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \end{array}\right]$ and the output is a three-dimensional vector $\left[\begin{array}{c}3x_1 - 2x_2 \\ x_1 + 2x_2 \\ 3x_2 \end{array}\right]$. As a shorthand, we will write

$$ \kT:{\mathbb R}^2\to{\mathbb R}^3 $$

to indicate that the inputs are two-dimensional vectors and the outputs are three-dimensional vectors.

Example 2.5.5

Suppose we have a function $\kT:{\mathbb R}^3\to{\mathbb R}^2$ that has the form

$$ \kT\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{array}\right]\right) = \begin{bmatrix} -4x_1 -x_2 + 2x_3 \\ x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

We may write

$$ \begin{aligned} \kT\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{array}\right]\right) & {}={} \begin{bmatrix} -4x_1 -x_2 + 2x_3 \\ x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 \\ \end{bmatrix} \\ & {}={} \left[\begin{array}{r}-4x_1 \\ x_1 \end{array}\right] + \left[\begin{array}{r}-x_2 \\ 2x_2 \end{array}\right] + \left[\begin{array}{r}2x_3 \\ -x_3 \end{array}\right] \\ & {}={} x_1\left[\begin{array}{r}-4 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] + x_2\left[\begin{array}{r}-1 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] + x_3\left[\begin{array}{r}2 \\ -1 \end{array}\right] \\ &{}={} \begin{bmatrix} -4 & -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ \end{bmatrix} \left[\begin{array}{r}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{array}\right]. \end{aligned} $$

This shows that $\kT$ is a matrix transformation $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ associated to the matrix $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ \end{bmatrix}.$

Let’s discuss a few of the issues that appear in this activity. First, notice that the shape of the matrix $\kA$ and the dimension of the input vector $\kvecx$ must be compatible if the product $\kA\kvecx$ is to be defined. In particular, if $\kA$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, $\kvecx$ needs to be an $n$-dimensional vector, and the resulting product $\kA\kvecx$ will be an $m$-dimensional vector. For the associated matrix transformation, we therefore write $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ meaning $\kT$ takes vectors in ${\mathbb R}^n$ as inputs and produces vectors in ${\mathbb R}^m$ as outputs. For instance, if

$$ \kA=\left[\begin{array}{rrrr} 4 & 0 & -3 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] $$

, then $\kT:{\mathbb R}^4\to{\mathbb R}^2$.

Second, we can often reconstruct the matrix $\kA$ if we only know some output values from its associated linear transformation $\kT$ by remembering that matrix-vector multiplication constructs linear combinations. For instance, if $\kA$ is an $m\times2$ matrix $\kA=\left[\begin{array}{rr} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \end{array}\right]$, then

$$ \kT\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]\right) = \left[\begin{array}{rr} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] = 1\kvecvone + 0\kvecvtwo = \kvecvone $$

. That is, we can find the first column of $\kA$ by evaluating $\kT\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]\right)$. Similarly, the second column of $\kA$ is found by evaluating $\kT\left(\left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]\right)$.

More generally, we will write the columns of the $n\times n$ identity matrix as

$$ \kveceone = \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{array}\right],~~~ \kvecetwo = \left[\begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{array}\right],~~~ \ldots,~~~ \htmlClass{kv-vector}{\mathbf{e}_n} = \left[\begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{array}\right] $$

so that

$$ \kT(\kvecej) = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo & \ldots & \kvecv_n \end{array}\right] ~\kvecej = \kvecv_j $$

. This means that the $j^{th}$ column of $\kA$ is found by evaluating $\kT(\kvecej)$. We record this fact in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.5.6

If $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ is a matrix transformation given by $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$, then the matrix $\kA$ has columns $\kT(\kvecej)$; that is,

$$ \kA = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr} \kT(\kveceone) & \kT(\kvecetwo) & \cdots & \kT(\htmlClass{kv-vector}{\mathbf{e}_n}) \end{array}\right] $$

.

In these examples, we glided over an important point: how do we know these functions $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ can be expressed as matrix transformations? We will take up this question in detail in the next section and not worry about it for now.

Composing matrix transformations

It sometimes happens that we want to combine matrix transformations by performing one and then another. In the last activity, for instance, we considered the matrix transformation where $\kT(\kvecx)$ is the result of rotating the two-dimensional vector $\kvecx$ by $90^\circ$. Now suppose we are interested in rotating that vector twice; that is, we take a vector $\kvecx$, rotate it by $90^\circ$ to obtain $\kT(\kvecx)$, and then rotate the result by $90^\circ$ again to obtain $\kT(\kT(\kvecx))$.

This process is called function composition and likely appeared in an earlier algebra course. For instance, if $g(x) = 2x + 1$ and $h(x) = x^2$, the composition of these functions obtained by first performing $g$ and then performing $h$ is denoted by

$$ (h\circ g)(x) = h(g(x)) = h(2x+1) = (2x+1)^2. $$

Composing matrix transformations is similar. Suppose that we have two matrix transformations, $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ and $\kS:{\mathbb R}^m\to{\mathbb R}^p$. Their associated matrices will be denoted by $\kA$ and $\kB$ so that $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ and $\kS(\kvecx) = \kB\kvecx$. If we apply $\kT$ to a vector $\kvecx$ to obtain $\kT(\kvecx)$ and then apply $\kS$ to the result, we have

$$ (\kS\circ \kT)(\kvecx) = \kS(\kT(\kvecx)) = \kS(\kA\kvecx) = \kB\kA\kvecx = (\kB\kA)\kvecx. $$

Notice that this implies that the composition $(\kS\circ \kT)$ is itself a matrix transformation and that the associated matrix is the product $\kB\kA$.

Proposition 2.5.8

If $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ and $\kS:{\mathbb R}^m\to{\mathbb R}^p$ are matrix transformations with associated matrices $\kA$ and $\kB$ respectively, then the composition $(\kS\circ \kT)$ is also a matrix transformation whose associated matrix is the product $\kB\kA$.

Notice that the matrix transformations must be compatible if they are to be composed. In particular, the vector $\kT(\kvecx)$, an $m$-dimensional vector, must be a suitable input vector for $\kS$, which means that the inputs to $\kS$ must be $m$-dimensional. In fact, this is the same condition we need to form the product $\kB\kA$ of their associated matrices, namely, that the number of columns of $\kB$ is the same as the number of rows of $\kA$.

Discrete Dynamical Systems

In Chapter 4, we will give considerable attention to a specific type of matrix transformation, which is illustrated in the next activity.

As we will see later, this type of situation occurs frequently. We have a vector $\kvecx$ that describes the state of some system; in this case, $\kvecx$ describes the distribution of trucks between the two locations at a particular time. Then there is a matrix transformation $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ that describes the state at some later time. We call $\kvecx$ the state vector and $\kT$ the transition function, as it describes the transition of the state vector from one time to the next.

Beginning with an initial state $\kvecxzero$, we would like to know how the state evolves over time. For instance,

$$ \begin{aligned} \kvecx_1 & = \kT(\kvecxzero)=\kA\kvecxzero \\ \kvecx_2 & = \kT(\kvecx_1) = (\kT\circ \kT)(\kvecxzero)= \kA^2\kvecxzero \\ \kvecx_3 & = \kT(\kvecx_2) = \kA^3\kvecxzero \end{aligned} $$

and so on.

We call this situation where the state of a system evolves from one time to the next according to the rule $\kvecx_{k+1}=\kA\kvecxk$ a discrete dynamical system. In Chapter 4, we will develop a theory that enables us to make long-term predictions about the evolution of the state vector.
