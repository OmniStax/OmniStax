Introduction

Matrix transformations, which we explored in the last section, allow us to describe certain functions $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$. In this section, we will demonstrate how matrix transformations provide a convenient way to describe geometric operations, such as rotations, reflections, and scalings. We will then explore how matrix transformations are used in computer animation.

The geometry of $2\times2$ matrix transformations

We have now seen how a few geometric operations, such as rotations and reflections, can be described using matrix transformations. The following activity shows, more generally, that matrix transformations can perform a variety of important geometric operations.

The previous activity presented some examples showing that matrix transformations can perform interesting geometric operations, such as rotations, scalings, and reflections. Before we go any further, we should explain why it is possible to represent these operations by matrix transformations. In fact, we ask more generally: what types of functions $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ are represented as matrix transformations?

The linearity of matrix-vector multiplication provides the key to answering this question. Remember that if $\kA$ is a matrix, $\kvecv$ and $\kvecw$ vectors, and $c$ a scalar, then

$$ \begin{aligned} \kA(c\kvecv) & {}={} c\kA\kvecv \\ \kA(\kvecv + \kvecw) & {}={} \kA\kvecv + \kA\kvecw\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

This means that a matrix transformation $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ satisfies the corresponding linearity property:

Summary · Linearity of Matrix Transformations
$$ \begin{aligned} \kT(c\kvecv) & {}={} c\kT(\kvecv) \\ \kT(\kvecv + \kvecw) & {}={} \kT(\kvecv) + \kT(\kvecw)\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

It turns out that, if $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ satisfies these two linearity properties, then we can find a matrix $\kA$ such that $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$. In fact, Proposition 2.5.6 tells us how to form $\kA$; we simply write

$$ \kA = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr} \kT(\kveceone) & \kT(\kvecetwo) & \ldots & \kT(\mathbf{e}_n) \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

We will now check that $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ using the linearity of $\kT$:

The result is the following proposition.

Proposition 2.6.3

The function $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ is a matrix transformation where $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ for some $m\times n$ matrix $\kA$ if and only if

$$ \begin{aligned} \kT(c\kvecv) & {}={} c\kT(\kvecv) \\ \kT(\kvecv + \kvecw) & {}={} \kT(\kvecv) + \kT(\kvecw)\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

In this case, $\kA$ is the matrix whose columns are $\kT(\kvecej)$; that is,

$$ \kA=\left[\begin{array}{rrrr} \kT(\kveceone) & \kT(\kvecetwo) & \ldots & \kT(\mathbf{e}_n) \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

Said simply, this proposition means that if we have a function $\kT:{\mathbb R}^n\to{\mathbb R}^m$ and can verify the two linearity properties stated in the proposition, then we know that $\kT$ is a matrix transformation. Let’s see how this works in practice.

Example 2.6.4

We will consider the function $\kT:{\mathbb R}^2\to{\mathbb R}^2$ that rotates a vector $\kvecx$ by $45^\circ$ in the counterclockwise direction to obtain $\kT(\kvecx)$ as seen in Figure 2.6.5.

We first need to know that $\kT$ can be represented by a matrix transformation, which means, by Proposition 2.6.3, that we need to verify the linearity properties:

$$ \begin{aligned} \kT(c\kvecv) & {}={} c\kT(\kvecv) \\ \kT(\kvecv + \kvecw) & {}={} \kT(\kvecv) + \kT(\kvecw)\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

The next two figures illustrate why these properties hold. For instance, Figure 2.6.6 shows the relationship between $\kT(\kvecv)$ and $\kT(c\kvecv)$ when $c$ is a scalar. In particular, scaling a vector and then rotating it is the same as rotating and then scaling it, which means that $\kT(c\kvecv) = c\kT(\kvecv)$.

Similarly, Figure 2.6.7 shows the relationship between $\kT(\kvecv+\kvecw)$, $\kT(\kvecv)$, and $\kT(\kvecw)$. Remember that the sum of two vectors is represented by the diagonal of the parallelogram defined by the two vectors. The rotation $\kT$ has the effect of rotating the parallelogram defined by $\kvecv$ and $\kvecw$ into the parallelogram defined by $\kT(\kvecv)$ and $\kT(\kvecw)$, explaining why $\kT(\kvecv+\kvecw) = \kT(\kvecv) + \kT(\kvecw)$.

Having verified these two properties, we now know that the function $\kT$ that rotates vectors by $45^\circ$ is a matrix transformation. We may therefore write it as $\kT(\kvecx) = \kA\kvecx$ where $\kA$ is the $2\times2$ matrix $\kA=\left[\begin{array}{rr} \kT(\kveceone) & \kT(\kvecetwo) \end{array}\right]$. The columns of this matrix, $\kT(\kveceone)$ and $\kT(\kvecetwo)$, are shown on the right of Figure 2.6.8.

Notice that $\kT(\kveceone)$ forms an isosceles right triangle, as shown in Figure 2.6.9. Since the length of $\kveceone$ is 1, the length of $\kT(\kveceone)$, the hypotenuse of the triangle, is also 1, and by Pythagoras’ theorem, the lengths of its legs are $1/\sqrt{2}$.

This leads to $\kT(\kveceone) = \left[\begin{array}{r}\frac1{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac1{\sqrt{2}} \end{array}\right]$. In the same way, we find that $\kT(\kvecetwo) = \left[\begin{array}{r}-\frac1{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac1{\sqrt{2}} \end{array}\right]$ so that the matrix $\kA$ is

$$ \kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$

You may wish to check this using the interactive diagram in the previous activity using the approximation $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.7$.

In this example, we found that $\kT$, a function describing a rotation in the plane, was in fact a matrix transformation by checking that

$$ \begin{aligned} \kT(c\kvecv) & {}={} c\kT(\kvecv) \\ \kT(\kvecv + \kvecw) & {}={} \kT(\kvecv) + \kT(\kvecw)\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

The same kind of thinking applies more generally to show that rotations, reflections, and scalings are matrix transformations. Similarly, we could revisit the functions in Activity 2.5.3 and verify that they are matrix transformations.

Matrix transformations and computer animation

Linear algebra plays a significant role in computer animation. We will now illustrate how matrix transformations and some of the ideas we have developed in this section are used by computer animators to create the illusion of motion in their characters.

Figure 2.6.10 shows a test character used by Pixar animators. On the left is the original definition of the character; on the right, we see that the character has been moved into a different pose. To make it appear that the character is moving, animators create a sequence of frames in which the character’s pose is modified slightly from one frame to the next often using matrix transformations.

Of course, realistic characters will be drawn in three dimensions. To keep things a little more simple, however, we will look at this two-dimensional character and devise matrix transformations that move them into different poses.

Of course, the first thing we may wish to do is simply move them to a different position in the plane, such as that shown in Figure 2.6.11. Motions like this are called translations.

This presents a problem because a matrix transformation $\kT:{\mathbb R}^2\to{\mathbb R}^2$ has the property that $\kT(\kveczero) = \kA\kveczero = \kveczero$. This means that a matrix transformation cannot move the origin of the coordinate plane. To address this restriction, animators use homogeneous coordinates, which are formed by placing the two-dimensional coordinate plane inside ${\mathbb R}^3$ as the plane $z=1$, as shown in Figure 2.6.12.

As a result, rather than describing points in the plane as vectors $\left[\begin{array}{r}x \\ y \end{array}\right]$, we describe them as three-dimensional vectors $\left[\begin{array}{r}x \\ y \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$. As we see in the next activity, this allows us to translate our character in the plane.
