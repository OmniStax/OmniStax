Another way to solve Ax = b

Up to this point, we have used the Gaussian elimination algorithm to find solutions to linear systems. We now investigate another way to find solutions to the equation $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ when the matrix $\kA$ has the same number of rows and columns. To get started, let’s look at some familiar examples.

Introduction

Invertible matrices

The preview activity began with a familiar type of equation, $3x = 5$, and asked for a strategy to solve it. One possible response is to divide both sides by 3. Instead, let’s rephrase this as multiplying by $3^{-1} = \frac 13$, the multiplicative inverse of 3.

Now that we are interested in solving equations of the form $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$, we might try to find a similar approach. Is there a matrix $\kAinv$ that plays the role of the multiplicative inverse of $\kA$? Of course, the real number $0$ does not have a multiplicative inverse so we probably shouldn’t expect every matrix to have a multiplicative inverse. We will see, however, that many do.

Definition 3.1.1

An $n\times n$ matrix $\kA$ is called invertible if there is a matrix $\kB$ such that $\kA\kB = \kIn$, where $\kIn$ is the $n\times n$ identity matrix. The matrix $\kB$ is called the inverse of $\kA$ and denoted $\kAinv$.

Notice that we only define invertibility for matrices that have the same number of rows and columns in which case we say that the matrix is square.

Example 3.1.2

Suppose that $\kA$ is the matrix that rotates two-dimensional vectors counterclockwise by $90^\circ$ and that $\kB$ rotates vectors by $-90^\circ$. We have

$$ \kA=\left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right],~~~ \kB=\left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

We can check that

$$ \kA\kB = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix} = \kI $$

which shows that $\kA$ is invertible and that $\kAinv=\kB$.

Notice that if we multiply the matrices in the opposite order, we find that $\kB\kA=\kI$, which says that $\kB$ is also invertible and that $\kBinv = \kA$. In other words, $\kA$ and $\kB$ are inverses of each other.

Example 3.1.3

We can reformulate this procedure for finding the inverse of a matrix. For the sake of convenience, suppose that $\kA$ is a $2\times2$ invertible matrix with inverse $\kB=\begin{bmatrix} \kvecbone & \kvecbtwo \end{bmatrix}$. Rather than solving the equations

$$ \kA\kvecbone = \left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right],~~~ \kA\kvecbtwo = \left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] $$

separately, we can solve them at the same time by augmenting $\kA$ by both vectors $\left[\begin{array}{r}1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$ and $\left[\begin{array}{r}0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and finding the reduced row echelon form.

For example, if $\kA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$, we form

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{rr|rr} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array} \right] \sim \left[ \begin{array}{rr|rr} 1 & 0 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & -1 \\ \end{array} \right]. $$

This shows that the matrix $\kB = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ is the inverse of $\kA$.

In other words, beginning with $\kA$, we augment by the identify and find the reduced row echelon form to determine $\kAinv$:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{r|r} \kA & \kI \\ \end{array} \right] \sim \left[ \begin{array}{r|r} \kI & \kAinv \\ \end{array} \right]. $$

In fact, this reformulation will always work. Suppose that $\kA$ is an invertible $n\times n$ matrix with inverse $\kB$. Suppose furthermore that $\kvecb$ is any $n$-dimensional vector and consider the equation $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$. We know that $x=\kB\kvecb$ is a solution because $\kA(\kB\kvecb) = (\kA\kB)\kvecb = \kI\kvecb = \kvecb.$

Proposition 3.1.4

If $\kA$ is an invertible matrix with inverse $\kB$, then any equation $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$ is consistent and $\kvecx = \kB\kvecb$ is a solution. In other words, the solution to $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$ is $\kvecx = \kAinv\kvecb$.

Notice that this is similar to saying that the solution to $3x=5$ is $x = \frac13\cdot 5$, as we saw in the preview activity.

Now since $\kA\kvecx=\kvecb$ is consistent for every vector $\kvecb$, the columns of $\kA$ must span ${\mathbb R}^n$ so there is a pivot position in every row. Since $\kA$ is also square, this means that the reduced row echelon form of $\kA$ is the identity matrix.

Proposition 3.1.5

The matrix $\kA$ is invertible if and only if the reduced row echelon form of $\kA$ is the identity matrix: $\kA\sim \kI$. In addition, we can find the inverse by augmenting $\kA$ by the identity and finding the reduced row echelon form:

$$ \left[ \begin{array}{r|r} \kA & \kI \\ \end{array} \right] \sim \left[ \begin{array}{r|r} \kI & \kAinv \\ \end{array} \right]. $$

You may have noticed that Proposition 3.1.4 says that the solution to the equation $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$ is $\kvecx = \kAinv\kvecb$. Indeed, we know that this equation has a unique solution because $\kA$ has a pivot position in every column.

It is important to remember that the product of two matrices depends on the order in which they are multiplied. That is, if $\kC$ and $\kD$ are matrices, then it sometimes happens that $\kC\kD \neq \kD\kC$. However, something fortunate happens when we consider invertibility. It turns out that if $\kA$ is an $n\times n$ matrix and that $\kA\kB=\kI$, then it is also true that $\kB\kA=\kI$. We have verified this in a few examples so far, and Exercise 3.1.12 explains why it always happens. This leads to the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1.6

If $\kA$ is a $n\times n$ invertible matrix with inverse $\kB$, then $\kB\kA=\kI$, which tells us that $\kB$ is invertible with inverse $\kA$. In other words,

$$ (\kAinv)^{-1} = \kA. $$

Solving equations with an inverse

If $\kA$ is an invertible matrix, then Proposition 3.1.4 shows us how to use $\kAinv$ to solve equations involving $\kA$. In particular, the solution to $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$ is $\kvecx = \kAinv\kvecb$.

The next proposition summarizes much of what we have found about invertible matrices.

Proposition 3.1.7 · Properties of invertible matrices
  • An $n\times n$ matrix $\kA$ is invertible if and only if $\kA\sim \kI$.
  • If $\kA$ is invertible, then the solution to the equation $\kA\kvecx = \kvecb$ is given by $\kvecx = \kAinv\kvecb$.
  • We can find $\kAinv$ by finding the reduced row echelon form of $\left[\begin{array}{r|r} \kA & \kI \end{array}\right]$; namely, $$ \left[\begin{array}{r|r} \kA & \kI \end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{r|r} \kI & \kAinv \end{array}\right]. $$
  • If $\kA$ and $\kB$ are two invertible $n\times n$ matrices, then their product $\kA\kB$ is also invertible and $(\kA\kB)^{-1} = \kBinv\kAinv$.

There is a simple formula for finding the inverse of a $2\times2$ matrix:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{rr} a & b \\ c & d \\ \end{array}\right]^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \left[\begin{array}{rr} d & -b \\ -c & a \\ \end{array}\right], $$

which can be easily checked. The condition that $\kA$ be invertible is, in this case, reduced to the condition that $ad-bc\neq 0$. We will understand this condition better once we have explored determinants in Section 3.4. There is a similar formula for the inverse of a $3\times 3$ matrix, but there is not a good reason to write it here.

Triangular matrices and Gaussian elimination

With some of the ideas we’ve developed, we can recast the Gaussian elimination algorithm in terms of matrix multiplication and invertibility. This will be especially helpful later when we consider determinants and LU factorizations. Triangular matrices will play an important role.

Definition 3.1.8

We say that a matrix $\kA$ is lower triangular if all its entries above the diagonal are zero. Similarly, $\kA$ is upper triangular if all the entries below the diagonal are zero.

For example, the matrix $\kL$ below is a lower triangular matrix while $\kU$ is an upper triangular one.

$$ \kL = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr} * & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ * & * & 0 & 0 \\ * & * & * & 0 \\ * & * & * & * \\ \end{array}\right], \hspace{24pt} \kU = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr} * & * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & * \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

We can develop a simple test to determine whether an $n\times n$ lower triangular matrix is invertible. Let’s use Gaussian elimination to find the reduced row echelon form of the lower triangular matrix

$$ \begin{aligned} \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & -2 & 0 \\ -3 & 4 & -4 \\ \end{array}\right] & {}\sim{} \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & -4 \\ \end{array}\right] \\ & {}\sim{} \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \\ \end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right]\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

Because the entries on the diagonal are nonzero, we find a pivot position in every row, which tells us that the matrix is invertible.

If, however, there is a zero entry on the diagonal, the matrix cannot be invertible. Considering the matrix below, we see that having a zero on the diagonal leads to a row without a pivot position.

$$ \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 4 & -4 \\ \end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & -4 \\ \end{array}\right] \sim \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right]\text{.} $$
Proposition 3.1.9

An $n\times n$ triangular matrix is invertible if and only if the entries on the diagonal are all nonzero.

The following are examples of matrices, known as elementary matrices, that perform the row operations on a matrix having three rows.

  • Replacement: Multiplying the second row by 3 and adding it to the third row is performed by $$ \kL = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$ We often use $\kL$ to describe these matrices because they are lower triangular.
  • Scaling: Multiplying the third row by 2 is performed by $$ \kS = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$
  • Interchange: Interchanging the first two rows is performed by $$ \kP = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

Example 3.1.10

Suppose we have

$$ \kA = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 3 & -2 \\ -3 & -6 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

For the forward substitution phase of Gaussian elimination, we perform a sequence of three replacement operations. The first replacement operation multiplies the first row by $3$ and adds the result to the second row. We can perform this operation by multiplying $\kA$ by the lower triangular matrix $\kL_1$ where

$$ \kL_1\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 3 & -2 \\ -3 & -6 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 3 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & -3 \\ 2 & 0 & -2 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

The next two replacement operations are performed by the matrices

$$ \kL_2 = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right], \hspace{24pt} \kL_3 = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] $$

so that

$$ \kL_3\kL_2\kL_1\kA = \kU = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & -3 \\ 0 & 0 & -4 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

Notice that the inverse of $\kL_1$ has the simple form:

$$ \kL_1 = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right], \hspace{24pt} \kL_1^{-1} = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

This says that if we want to undo the operation of multiplying the first row by $3$ and adding to the second row, we should multiply the first row by $-3$ and add it to the second row. That is the effect of $\kL_1^{-1}$.

Notice that we now have $\kL_3\kL_2\kL_1\kA = \kU$, which gives

$$ \begin{aligned} (\kL_3\kL_2\kL_1)\kA & = \kU \\ (\kL_3\kL_2\kL_1)^{-1}(\kL_3\kL_2\kL_1)\kA & = (\kL_3\kL_2\kL_1)^{-1}\kU \\ \kA & = (\kL_3\kL_2\kL_1)^{-1}\kU = \kL\kU\\ \end{aligned} $$

where $\kL$ is the lower triangular matrix

$$ \kL = (\kL_3\kL_2\kL_1)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & -2 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}. $$

This way of writing $\kA=\kL\kU$ as the product of a lower and an upper triangular matrix is known as an $LU$ factorization of $\kA$, and its usefulness will be explored in Section 5.1.
