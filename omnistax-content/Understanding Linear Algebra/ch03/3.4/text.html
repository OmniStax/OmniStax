A numerical test for invertibility

As invertibility plays a central role in this chapter, we need a criterion that tells us when a matrix is invertible. We already know that a square matrix is invertible if and only if it is row equivalent to the identity matrix. In this section, we will develop a second, numerical criterion that tells us when a square matrix is invertible.

Introduction

To begin, let’s consider a $2\times2$ matrix $\kA$ whose columns are vectors $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$. We have frequently drawn the vectors and studied the linear combinations they form using a figure such as Figure 3.4.1.

Notice how the linear combinations form a set of congruent parallelograms in the plane. In this section, we will use the area of these parallelograms to define a numerical quantity called the determinant that tells us whether the matrix $\kA$ is invertible.

To recall, the area of parallelogram is found by multiplying the length of one side by the perpendicular distance to its parallel side. Using the notation in the figure, the area of the parallelogram is $bh$.

Determinants of 2 × 2 matrices

We will begin by defining the determinant of a $2\times2$ matrix $\kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \end{array}\right]$. First, however, we need to define the orientation of an ordered pair of vectors. As shown in Figure 3.4.2, an ordered pair of vectors $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ is called positively oriented if the angle, measured in the counterclockwise direction, from $\kvecvone$ to $\kvecvtwo$ is less than $180^\circ$; we say the pair is negatively oriented if it is more than $180^\circ$.

Definition 3.4.3

Suppose a $2\times2$ matrix $\kA$ has columns $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$. If the pair of vectors is positively oriented, then the determinant of $\kA$, denoted $\det(\kA)$, is the area of the parallelogram formed by $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$. If the pair is negatively oriented, then $\det(\kA)$ is -1 times the area of the parallelogram.

Example 3.4.4

Consider the determinant of the identity matrix

$$ \kI = \left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} \kveceone & \kvecetwo \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

As seen on the left of Figure 3.4.5, the vectors $\kvecvone = \kveceone$ and $\kvecvtwo=\kvecetwo$ form a positively oriented pair. Since the parallelogram they form is a $1\times1$ square, we have $\det(\kI) = 1.$

Now consider the matrix

$$ \kA = \left[\begin{array}{rr} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{rr} \kvecvone & \kvecvtwo \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

As seen on the right of Figure 3.4.5, the vectors $\kvecvone$ and $\kvecvtwo$ form a negatively oriented pair. The parallelogram they define is a $2\times1$ rectangle so we have $\det(\kA) = -2$.

Later in this section, we will learn an algebraic technique for computing determinants. In the meantime, we will simply note that we can define determinants for $n\times n$ matrices by measuring the volume of a box defined by the columns of the matrix, even if this box resides in ${\mathbb R}^n$ for some very large $n$.

For example, the columns of a $3\times3$ matrix $\kA$ will form a parallelpiped, like the one shown here, and there is a means by which we can classify sets of such vectors as either positively or negatively oriented. Therefore, we can define the determinant $\det(\kA)$ in terms of the volume of the parallelpiped, but we will not worry about the details here.

Though the previous activity deals with determinants of $2\times2$ matrices, it illustrates some important properties of determinants that are true more generally.

  • If $\kA$ is a triangular matrix, then $\det(\kA)$ equals the product of the entries on the diagonal. For example,

    $$ \det\left[\begin{array}{rr} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \\ \end{array}\right] = 2\cdot 3 = 6, $$

    since the two parallelograms in Figure 3.4.7 have equal area.

  • We also saw that

    $$ \det \left[\begin{array}{rr} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ \end{array}\right] = -1 $$

    because the columns form a negatively oriented pair. You may remember from Subsection 3.1.4 that a matrix such as this is obtained by interchanging two rows of the identity matrix.

  • The determinant satisfies a multiplicative property, which says that

    $$ \det(\kA\kB) = \det(\kA)\det(\kB). $$

    Rather than simply thinking of the determinant as the area of a parallelogram, we may also think of it as a factor by which areas are scaled under the matrix transformation defined by the matrix. Applying the matrix transformation defined by $\kB$ will scale area by $\det(\kB)$. If we then compose $\kB$ with the matrix transformation defined by $\kA$, area will scale a second time by the factor $\det(\kA)$. The net effect is that the matrix transformation defined by $\kA\kB$ scales area by $\det(\kA)\det(\kB)$ so that $\det(\kA\kB)=\det(\kA)\det(\kB)$.

Proposition 3.4.8

The determinant satisfies these properties:

  • The determinant of a triangular matrix equals the product of its diagonal entries.
  • If $\kP$ is obtained by interchanging two rows of the identity matrix, then $\det(\kP) = -1$.
  • $\det(\kA\kB) = \det(\kA)\det(\kB)$.

Determinants and invertibility

Perhaps the most important property of determinants also appeared in the previous activity. We saw that when the columns of the matrix $\kA$ are linearly dependent, the parallelogram formed by those vectors folds down onto a line. For instance, if $\kA=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & -2 \\ \end{bmatrix}$, then the resulting parallelogram, as shown in Figure 3.4.9, has zero area, which means that $\det(\kA)=0$.

The condition that the columns of $\kA$ are linearly dependent is precisely the same as the condition that $\kA$ is not invertible. This leads us to believe that $\kA$ is not invertible if and only if its determinant is zero. The following proposition expresses this thought.

Proposition 3.4.10

The matrix $\kA$ is invertible if and only if $\det(\kA) \neq 0$.

To understand this proposition more fully, let’s remember that the matrix $\kA$ is invertible if and only if it is row equivalent to the identity matrix $\kI$. We will therefore consider how the determinant changes when we perform row operations on a matrix. Along the way, we will discover an effective means to compute the determinant.

In Subsection 3.1.4, we saw how to describe the three row operations, scaling, interchange, and replacement, using matrix multiplication. If we perform a row operation on the matrix $\kA$ to obtain the matrix $\kAprime$, we would like to relate $\det(\kA)$ and $\det(\kAprime)$. To do so, remember that

  • Scalings are performed by multiplying a matrix $\kA$ by a diagonal matrix, such as

    $$ \kS = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right], $$

    which has the effect of multiplying the second row of $\kA$ by $3$ to obtain $\kAprime$. Since $\kS$ is diagonal, we know that its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries so that $\det(\kS) = 3$. This means that $\kAprime=\kS\kA$ and therefore

    $$ \det(\kAprime)=\det(\kS)\det(\kA) = 3\det(\kA). $$

    In general, if we scale a row of $\kA$ by $k$, we have $\det(\kAprime) = k\det(\kA)$.

  • Interchanges are performed by matrices such as

    $$ \kP = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right], $$

    which has the effect of interchanging the first and second rows of $\kA$. As we saw in Proposition 3.4.8, $\det(\kP) = -1$. Therefore, when $\kP\kA=\kAprime$, we have

    $$ \det(\kAprime)=\det(\kP) \det(\kA) = -\det(\kA). $$

    In other words, $\det(\kAprime) = -\det(\kA)$ when we perform an interchange.

  • Row replacement operations are performed by matrices such as

    $$ \kR = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 0 & 1 \\ \end{array}\right], $$

    which multiplies the first row by $-2$ and adds the result to the third row. Since this is a lower triangular matrix, we know that the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries, which says that $\det(\kR) = 1$. This means that when $\kR\kA = \kAprime$, we have $\det(\kAprime)=\det(\kR)\det(\kA) = \det(\kA)$. In other words, a row replacement does not change the determinant.

Proposition 3.4.11 · The effect of row operations on the determinant
  • If $\kAprime$ is obtained from $\kA$ by scaling a row by $k$, then $\det(\kAprime) = k\det(\kA)$.
  • If $\kAprime$ is obtained from $\kA$ by interchanging two rows, then $\det(\kAprime) = -\det(\kA)$.
  • If $\kAprime$ is obtained from $\kA$ by performing a row replacement operation, then $\det(\kAprime) = \det(\kA)$.

As seen in this activity, row operations can be used to compute the determinant of a matrix. More specifically, applying the forward substitution phase of Gaussian elimination to the matrix $\kA$ leads us to an upper triangular matrix $\kU$ so that $\kA\sim \kU$.

We know that $\kU$ is invertible when all of its diagonal entries are nonzero. We also know that $\det(\kU)\neq 0$ under the same condition. This tells us $\kU$ is invertible if and only if $\det(\kU) \neq 0$.

Now if $\det(\kA)\neq 0$, we also have $\det(\kU)\neq 0$ since applying a sequence of row operations to $\kA$ only multiplies the determinant by a nonzero number. It then follows that $\kU$ is invertible so $\kU\sim \kI$. Therefore, we also know that $\kA\sim \kI$ and so $\kA$ must also be invertible.

This explains Proposition 3.4.10 and so we know that $\kA$ is invertible if and only if $\det(\kA)\neq 0$.

Finally, notice that if $\kA$ is invertible, we have $\kAinv\kA = \kI$, which tells us that

$$ \det(\kAinv\kA) = \det(\kAinv)\det(\kA) = 1. $$

Therefore, $\det(\kAinv) = 1/\det(\kA)$.

Proposition 3.4.12

If $\kA$ is an invertible matrix, then $\det(\kAinv) = 1/\det(\kA)$.

Cofactor expansions

We now have a technique for computing the determinant of a matrix using row operations. There is another way to compute determinants, using what are called cofactor expansions, that will be important for us in the next chapter. We will describe this method here.

To begin, the determinant of a $2\times2$ matrix is

$$ \det\left[\begin{array}{rr} a & b \\ c & d \\ \end{array}\right] = ad-bc. $$

With a little bit of work, it can be shown that this number is the same as the signed area of the parallelogram we introduced earlier.

Using a cofactor expansion to find the determinant of a more general $n\times n$ matrix is a little more work so we will demonstrate it with an example.

Example 3.4.13

We illustrate how to use a cofactor expansion to find the determinant of $\kA$ where

$$ \kA = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 1 & -1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & -6 \\ 3 & -1 & 10 \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

To begin, we choose one row or column. It doesn’t matter which we choose because the result will be the same in any case. Here, we choose the second row

$$ \left[\begin{array}{rrr} \color{lightgray}{1} & \color{lightgray}{-1} & \color{lightgray}{2} \\ -2 & 2 & -6 \\ \color{lightgray}{3} & \color{lightgray}{-1} & \color{lightgray}{10} \\ \end{array}\right]. $$

The determinant will be found by creating a sum of terms, one for each entry in the row we have chosen. For each entry in the row, we form its term by multiplying

  • $(-1)^{i+j}$ where $i$ and $j$ are the row and column numbers, respectively, of the entry,
  • the entry itself, and
  • the determinant of the entries left over when we have crossed out the row and column containing the entry.

Since we are computing the determinant of this matrix

$$ \left[\begin{array}{rrr} \color{gray}{1} & \color{gray}{-1} & \color{gray}{2} \\ -2 & 2 & -6 \\ \color{gray}{3} & \color{gray}{-1} & \color{gray}{10} \\ \end{array}\right] $$

using the second row, the entry in the first column of this row is $-2$. Let’s see how to form the term from this entry.

The term itself is $-2$, and the matrix that is left over when we cross out the second row and first column is

$$ \left[\begin{array}{rrr} \color{gray}{1} & {-1} & {2} \\ \color{gray}{-2} & \color{gray}{2} & \color{gray}{-6} \\ \color{gray}{3} & {-1} & {10} \\ \end{array}\right] $$

whose determinant is

$$ \det\left[\begin{array}{rr} -1 & 2 \\ -1 & 10 \\ \end{array}\right] = -1(10) - 2 (-1) = -8. $$

Since this entry is in the second row and first column, the term we construct is $(-1)^{2+1}(-2)(-8) = -16$.

Putting this together, we find the determinant to be

$$ \begin{aligned} \left[\begin{array}{rrr} {1} & {-1} & {2} \\ -2 & {2} & {-6} \\ {3} & {-1} & {10} \\ \end{array}\right] {}={} & (-1)^{2+1}(-2)\det\left[\begin{array}{rr} -1 & 2 \\ -1 & 10 \\ \end{array}\right] \\ & {}+{} (-1)^{2+2}(2)\det\left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 10 \\ \end{array}\right] \\ & {}+{} (-1)^{2+3}(-6)\det\left[\begin{array}{rr} 1 & -1 \\ 3 & -1 \\ \end{array}\right] \\ \\ {}={} & (-1)(-2)(-1(10)-2(-1)) \\ & + (1)(2)(1(10)-2(3)) \\ & + (-1)(-6)((1)(-1)-(-1)3) \\ \\ {}={} & -16 + 8 + 12 \\ {}={} & 4. \\ \end{aligned} $$

Notice that this agrees with the determinant that we found for this matrix using row operations in the last activity.
